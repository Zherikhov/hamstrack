## Auth rate limiting (2026-07-14)

`common.ratelimit` — two in-memory mechanisms (single-node; move to Redis if Cloud scales out), both configurable via `app.rate-limit.*` (env `RATE_LIMIT_*`, `enabled` default `true`): **per-IP fixed window** (default 15 req/min) across login/register/verify-email/resend-verification/forgot-password/reset-password, enforced by `AuthRateLimitFilter` — registered via `FilterRegistrationBean` with explicit URL patterns (NOT `@Component`, see filter gotcha) at highest precedence, POST only (`/refresh`+`/logout` excluded: cookie-driven, called on every page load); and **per-account exponential login backoff** (5 fails → 30s, doubling, cap 15 min; success resets) in `AuthService.login`, keyed by submitted email even for unknown accounts (no enumeration via the limiter). Both raise `RateLimitedException` (429 + `Retry-After`, dedicated `GlobalExceptionHandler` handler). Client IP = rightmost `X-Forwarded-For` entry (Caddy ≥2.5 discards client-supplied XFF; only Caddy is exposed on prod) else `remoteAddr`. Counter eviction via `@Scheduled` (`@EnableScheduling` on the app class). Tests: `AuthRateLimitTest` (Boot 4 note: `AutoConfigureMockMvc` lives in `org.springframework.boot.webmvc.test.autoconfigure`).

## Invitation ceilings — the mailer, not the table (HD-190, 2026-08-27)

`WorkspaceService.inviteMember` sent mail to any address a caller typed, with no budget of any kind. Public signup is on in production and nothing bounds how many workspaces one user creates, so the account costs one throwaway mailbox, and the mail carries our SPF/DKIM. Three ceilings now, spent **after** the workspace is resolved and after the 403/422/409 checks — so unlike every other 429 in this API, one from here is only ever seen by a proven member. They are deliberately **not** a `PrincipalThrottleInterceptor`: a recipient-keyed refusal spent before the controller resolves anything would answer a cross-tenant question to a non-member, where the tenancy contract requires 404.

- **Per-sender volume** (`InviteSenderVolumeBudget`) — hour + day fixed windows on the principal, **in memory**, node-local. Two windows because the two losses have two units: the hour bounds a burst and **the day is the quota control** (100/day against a 3000/month provider quota). Deliberately **no per-minute window** — any per-minute ceiling loose enough for a human is ~28 800/day.
- **Per-(sender, recipient) cooldown** and **global per-recipient daily cap** (`RecipientMailThrottle` + `MailThrottlePolicy`) — **persisted** in `mail_send_events` (V21), FK-free, append-only, not workspace-scoped, read **only** as aggregate counts (`MailSendEventRepository` deliberately does not extend `JpaRepository` and has no method returning an entity or an address). Exact under concurrency via `pg_advisory_xact_lock(hashtext(recipient_key))` after `LockTimeout.applyToCurrentTransaction()`. **The first limiters in the product that are cluster-wide** — their state is in PostgreSQL, not in a process.
- **They count the INBOX, not the spelling.** `mail_send_events` carries both `recipient_email` (exactly as submitted, what a refusal echoes) and `recipient_key` (`MailAddresses.throttleKey`: `+tag` stripped, quoted local parts unquoted, domain punycoded, Gmail dots and `googlemail.com` folded — published delivery facts and standard normalisations only, never confusables), and every ceiling compares the key. Keyed on the lower-cased address alone the whole feature was decorative — `victim+1@`, `victim+2@`, `v.i.c.t.i.m@googlemail.com` are distinct strings reaching one human, so the attack was re-spelled for one keystroke. **For a ceiling, over-folding is fail-safe** (an extra match refuses sooner); on the invite *redemption* path (HD-120) the direction of harm is the opposite and addresses are compared exactly. Fail-safe is not free, and the cost differs per ceiling: an over-fold costs the *cooldown* an honest sender a wait they did not earn, but costs the sender-invariant *daily cap* a slot belonging to a different, innocent person — which is the bound, and the reason the folding rules admit no heuristics. The key is derived inside the throttle so no call site can forget it, and mail always goes to the submitted address.
- **The daily cap counts "own sends one each, other senders once each."** Counting raw sends made it a weapon: one account, five legal spaced sends, and no workspace on the instance could invite that named person for the rest of the day. Counting only distinct senders would let one account ring the doorbell all day. Both halves together keep the spec's arithmetic (five throwaway accounts still trip it at the sixth) while capping one account at the number itself and at one slot for everyone else. The hybrid's own cost is **quadratic in the cap**: N colluding accounts deliver `N × (cap − N + 1)` messages into one inbox per day, `floor((cap+1)² / 4)` at worst — 9/day at the default 5, 30/day at 10 — so raising the number buys more than it looks like. Its `Retry-After` **coarsens the deadline, not the remaining duration**: rounding the duration re-anchors the quantum to `now`, so the emitted number steps down onto the hidden instant and ~10 probes recover it. Rounding the deadline makes `now + Retry-After` identical at every probe, which means the emitted number is *not* a round multiple of the quantum — a value that is one is the fingerprint of the defeatable variant. It raises the price of another tenant's send instant rather than bounding the disclosure (waiting for the refusal to lift still reveals it); the **one-bit** claim belongs to the refusal's wording. The cooldown's wait is left exact because that instant is the caller's own.
- **Why persisted, and it is not durability** (ADR-0015): derived from `workspace_invites` the cooldown would be cleared by three existing paths, and the decisive one is that `declineInvite` performs a `DELETE` — a victim declining an unwanted invitation would unlock the attacker's next send.
- **`email_type` from day one**, and the table is named for mail rather than invitations: HD-202 (per-address throttle on the auth mailers) is the same key and the same problem, and it landed as one `MailThrottlePolicy` bean and one call site per auth mailer, with no migration — see below, including the places it did NOT match this forecast (the refusal is silent on two of the three endpoints, the volume window became per policy, and a third door had to exist because one mail type meets two endpoints with opposite disclosure contracts). Anonymous senders share one cooldown bucket per address (`countRecentForAnonymous`).
- **Not built here:** the per-workspace cap on outstanding invitations (spec §6.4 / Q1, owner's call). Seam documented on `WorkspaceService.inviteMember`: it belongs between the already-a-member check and the throttle, is a **409 stock cap** and **not** under `app.rate-limit.enabled`, and its prerequisite is a pending-invite list + revoke endpoint — without those its refusal prescribes an action its reader cannot perform.

**Config:** `app.invites.max-per-sender-per-hour|-per-day` (20/100), `app.invites.recipient-cooldown-minutes` (60), `app.invites.max-per-recipient-per-day` (5), `app.invites.event-retention-days` (7) — env `INVITE_*`, all `@Validated` (out of range or empty fails startup; `0` is out of range on every one) — plus a **cross-property check**: the retention must outlast the **wider of the two ceiling windows** — the configurable cooldown *and* the fixed 24 h window of the daily cap — or the hourly sweep deletes rows inside a live window and silently shortens that ceiling to the retention. Hence `event-retention-days` has a floor of **2**, not 1: one day is *exactly* the daily window, and exact is not cover across replicas whose sweep clocks differ. Master switch `app.rate-limit.enabled`, which reaches every limiter that *has* an off switch — the backlog-rebalance cooldown (`IssueRankService`) has none and is deliberately outside it. Identical in `dc` and `cloud`, no profile override — a DC install with public signup on has exactly the Cloud abuse profile, so a profile-keyed default would protect Cloud and leave the exposed install loose. The switch turns off the **ceilings**, not the bookkeeping: events are still recorded and swept, because they are the operator's only answer to "who" after a volume alert. **Metrics:** three `RateLimitKind`s (`invite_sender_volume` / `invite_recipient_cooldown` / `invite_recipient_daily`) plus one INFO line per allowed send carrying the recipient's **domain only**. **Alerts:** `MailDailyVolumeHigh`, `InviteVolumeUnaccepted`, `InviteThrottleTripping` — ranked in that order, because a rule built on refusals cannot see an abuser who stays under the ceilings. Spec: `docs/design/invite-budget-proposal.md`.

### Auth-mail ceilings (HD-202, 2026-09-02)

`POST /api/auth/forgot-password`, `POST /api/auth/resend-verification` and `POST /api/auth/register` were pure "look the address up and send", bounded only by the per-IP auth window. A budget keyed on where a request came from can always be widened by coming from somewhere else, so the account under attack is what is counted. The gap looked survivable only while `RATE_LIMIT_TRUST_FORWARDED_FOR` was not reaching the production container and the whole internet shared one bucket; HD-199 delivered the flag and made the key **one per visitor**, i.e. unlimited buckets a proxy pool can point at one inbox.

- **Reuses `RecipientMailThrottle`** — the invitation mechanism, the same `mail_send_events` table, **no migration** (`email_type` was there from day one for this). One `MailThrottlePolicy` bean and one call site per auth mailer (`AuthMailThrottleConfig`, `AuthService`), plus the emptying of `MailThrottleCoverageTest.EXEMPT`, which is what made the merge verifiable — an exemption that survives its own closing is a claim nobody re-reads.
- **The refusal is SILENT** (`MailThrottlePolicy.Refusal.SILENT`, entry point `RecipientMailThrottle.allowAnonymousSend`): no mail, unchanged `200`, unchanged body, no `429`, no log line. A visible refusal would not leak account existence — the ceiling is spent **before** the account lookup and recorded either way — but it would tell anybody, for free, that *somebody* asked for a reset at that address a minute ago. The refusal metrics are consequently the **only** witness this control has.
- **The ceiling is spent before the lookup**, which is where HD-202 and HD-208 meet: spent after it, or only on the branch that really sends, it would answer "registered / not registered" — a fix shaped exactly like the oracle both endpoints exist to refuse — and it would reintroduce the known-vs-unknown timing difference HD-208 removed.
- **The volume window is per policy now, and the widths differ**: a day for invitations, **15 minutes** for password reset, **an hour** for verification and for registration. A ceiling on a *recovery* flow is also a denial of it — anybody can type a victim's address into forgot-password — so a window's width is how long a named person can be kept from resetting their own password, silently. But **sustained denial is achievable at every width**, so the width buys no prevention, only attacker effort (1 req/12 min at 5-per-hour against 1 req/3 min at 5-per-quarter-hour). What it really buys is the mail bound; what it costs is the length of a **hit-and-run** lockout, which is the common case — five requests fired once buy the whole window. 20 reset mails/hour into one inbox is a nuisance and stays a factor of 3 under the deliverability number; an hour of silent lockout is not. Verification keeps the hour because it locks nobody out of an account they hold; registration keeps it because that is the flow most exposed to one inbox reached through many spellings, so the tighter mail bound is worth more there. Side effect: `InviteProperties`' hand-copied `1440` is gone; it now reads `MailThrottlePolicy.MAX_CEILING_WINDOW`, which the policy constructor also enforces as the ceiling on every window, and the invite cooldown's own `@Max` came down to **1439** because that constructor now refuses a cooldown that merely *equals* its window.
- **`POST /api/auth/register` spends a per-inbox budget of its own (`EmailType.REGISTRATION_VERIFICATION`), and the first cut's exemption was the bug this ticket exists to delete.** It shared the verification bucket until final review, which was itself a hole: `resend-verification` records **before** the account lookup and unconditionally (it must, or the row is the existence oracle), so five requests an hour at an address with **no account** sent no mail, logged nothing above DEBUG, and denied signup to every spelling of that inbox — free, silent, refillable for ever. Separate buckets make filling register's ceiling cost a real mail the victim sees and a `PENDING` row an admin can find; the price is a third bucket in the per-inbox bound, which is a **sum of rates and not a multiple of the cap** — at the default 5 that is 20/h of reset mail (a quarter-hour window refilling four times), 5/h of resend-verification and 5/h of registration, i.e. **30 an hour into one inbox**. The written reason — "registration produces at most one message per address, the second attempt is a `409`" — is true per *address*, and `MailAddresses.throttleKey` exists precisely because an address is not the unit of harm: `users.email` is unique on the **spelling** (`lower(email)`, V23) and the ceiling counts the **inbox**. With public signup on, `victim+1@`, `victim+2@`, `v.i.ctim@googlemail.com` were three rows, three `201`s, three mails, one inbox, and the throttle was never consulted — the one-keystroke re-spelling defeat, reproduced against the one door left off the mechanism built to close it. It also bought a bcrypt-12 (~370 ms) per message and littered `users` with PENDING rows. Register now refuses the **registration** (above the INSERT, so nothing is stranded) with a **`429`**, which is available there and nowhere else in this feature because register already discloses address existence through its own `409`. The budget is emphatically **not** shared with resend-verification — sharing it is the hole this bullet opens with, and a pre-split draft of this sentence claimed the opposite right here. What the split does *not* buy: denial of `resend-verification` itself is still free, silent and refillable (5/h at an inbox holds that inbox's own resend for the hour, sends no mail, logs nothing above DEBUG). Only register was rescued, and it was rescued because that denial is the unrecoverable one — somebody who cannot register has no account, no other route in, and nothing an admin can see.
  - The shape is declared on the policy, not chosen at the call site: `MailThrottlePolicy.Refusal.RESPONDS_429_WHERE_ENDPOINT_DISCLOSES` plus a third door, `RecipientMailThrottle.requireAndRecordWhereEndpointDiscloses`. **Each door admits exactly one `Refusal` constant** — while the guards read "not the opposite one", a type entitled to the anonymous 429 was also admissible through the plain `requireAndRecord`, which would have made `resend-verification` answer 429 while passing every guard and every seal. What no type can guard is *where* the door is called from, so its **set of call sites is sealed** by `AuthMailDoorsTest` **down to the enclosing method** (all three auth flows live in `AuthService`, so a file-granular seal watched nothing), which also asserts the throws — both reviewers named those the most load-bearing lines in the change and nothing had asserted them.
  - The ordering at the call site is deliberate and was argued **against** the review that asked for the opposite: the bcrypt happens **before** the ceiling. Spending first would save a refused caller one hash but would hold the recipient advisory lock across ~370 ms of caller-controlled work, which `RecipientMailThrottle`'s standing rule forbids. The saving is also illusory — a caller who wants CPU uses a fresh inbox each time, which is a fresh bucket and is always allowed.
- **The refusal metric cannot see the attack this feature exists to bound, so a gauge was added.** `rateLimitHit` fires only on refusal, and the denial-of-recovery attacker is never refused: pacing exactly as slots age out keeps the bucket full and every request allowed. `hamstrack.mail.anonymous_recipient_max` (`AnonymousMailConcentration`, 5-minute refresh, 6-hour window) reports how much anonymous auth mail reached the single busiest `recipient_key` — a quantity, not a refusal, and **carrying no address**, because a Grafana alert would mail its series labels to the operator. Alert `MailRecipientConcentration`; the address is looked up with the `mail_send_events` query in `docs/self-hosting.md`.
- **Anonymous rows have their own, shorter cutoff** (`AuthMailProperties.ANONYMOUS_EVENT_RETENTION`, 2 days, vs the invite retention's default 7) — precisely, their lifetime is `min` of the two, because the general sweep carries no sender predicate and reaches them as well; at the lowest legal `INVITE_EVENT_RETENTION_DAYS` the two coincide, which `MailSendEventRetention` now asserts at startup rather than leaving to two independently derived numbers. the long window is bought to answer *who*, and an anonymous row has no sender to name, while it is the one row shape an unauthenticated stranger can force the instance to write. The allowed-send INFO line drops to DEBUG for those rows for the same reason — a forensic trail an anonymous caller can turn into unbounded Loki ingest is itself the flooding vector. The advisory lock is keyed on `email_type` + `recipient_key`, not the address alone, so an anonymous `forgot-password` no longer serialises against a tenant's invitation to the same address.
- **Residual, stated rather than hidden, and now stated where an operator meets it:** an attacker willing to spend the cap every window keeps that address's recovery mail suppressed indefinitely, from any IP, never refused. No per-address ceiling can be built that cannot be filled; the choice is only how long a *burst* lasts and how visible the sustained version is. Both halves are in `docs/self-hosting.md` with the two queries that see them. The construction that would end it — a challenge (CAPTCHA / proof-of-work) once an address is over its cheap budget, shown for every address so the uniform-response contract survives — is a follow-up ticket, not built here.

**Config:** `app.auth-mail.recipient-cooldown-minutes` (1, range 1–14 = one minute inside the narrowest window, because the guard rejects a cooldown that merely *equals* its window — at equality the cap is unreachable) and `app.auth-mail.max-per-recipient-per-window` (5, range 1–1000, **per bucket, and there are three**) — env `AUTH_MAIL_*`, `@Validated`, no "unlimited" value, one pair applied to every auth mailer with **separate buckets per `email_type`**. Cluster-wide and exact (state in PostgreSQL): N replicas allow the configured number, not N × it. **Metrics:** six `RateLimitKind`s (`password_reset_`, `verification_`, `registration_verification_` × `recipient_cooldown|_window`) plus the `hamstrack.mail.anonymous_recipient_max` gauge and its freshness companion `..._max_age_seconds` — the gauge is last-write-wins, so a failing query **freezes** it at a quiet value rather than clearing it, and the age is the only thing that can tell the two apart. The age is **seeded at process start and has no "never refreshed" sentinel**: it used to publish `-1` until the first success, which put the worst state (a refresh that never succeeds at all — V25 unapplied, a persistent timeout) permanently *below* its own alert threshold, with both series present so `NoData`/`Error` could not help either. **Alerts:** `AuthMailThrottleTripping` (loud attacker), `MailRecipientVolumeCapReached` (low-threshold companion on the volume kinds only), `MailRecipientConcentration` (the quiet one), `MailConcentrationGaugeStale` (the witness's own witness). The last two depart from the file's `noDataState`/`execErrState` = `OK` default, and the rule for departing is a **category, not a count** — any rule whose failure mode is *absence* rather than noise, i.e. any rule watching a single-witness signal. Indexed by `V25`'s partial `(created_at, recipient_key) WHERE sender_user_id IS NULL`, without which the gauge's query slows in proportion to the flood it watches. The rules select refusal kinds by **naming convention** (`.*_recipient_(cooldown|window|daily)`) rather than by a list, and `MailThrottleCoverageTest.everyRecipientKindIsSelectedByTheAlertRules` reads those regexes out of `rules.yml` and fails if a policy declares a kind no rule would match.

Every user gets a "Demo Workspace" with a "Demo Project" (key `DEMO`, 20 dev-team-style issues — 2 epics with children, mixed types/statuses/priorities/due dates, some assigned to the user) seeded on their **first successful authentication**. `common.seed.DemoDataService.seedOnFirstLogin(userId)` is called from `AuthController` after `verify-email`, `login` **and** `refresh` (refresh matters: after a wipe, users with a live session never hit `/login`). Seeding reuses `WorkspaceService`/`ProjectService`/`IssueService`, so all normal invariants hold. Toggle: `app.demo.seed-on-first-login` (env `DEMO_SEED_ON_FIRST_LOGIN`, default `true`).

**Wiping an instance's user data (operator tool — not a policy).** Hamstrack Cloud does not do this any more — the reset policy was retired by HD-195 (ADR-0022, `docs/adr/0022-cloud-data-not-reset.md` — not published yet; the rule below is the part you need). The procedure below survives as an operator tool for a local, demo or self-hosted instance whose owner wants a clean slate. **Never as a versioned Flyway migration:** anything placed in the migration chain runs on every installation that upgrades, so a wipe committed there destroys every self-hosted installation's user data on its next routine `docker compose pull && up -d`, silently. Run it out-of-band instead — `psql -f wipe.sql` against the one instance being wiped, by that instance's operator. The block, which also re-arms demo seeding: `DELETE FROM issues;` (first — `issues.workspace_id` has no cascade), `DELETE FROM workspaces;`, `DELETE FROM notifications;` (belt-and-braces since `V20` — `notifications.workspace_id` cascades from `workspaces`, so the preceding delete already takes them; the statement is harmless and still covers any future row whose workspace outlives the reset), `UPDATE users SET demo_seeded_at = NULL;`. Do **not** rewrite existing migration files — Flyway checksum validation would fail on every already-migrated DB (prod, local) unless its volume is dropped. Attachment blobs are orphaned by a reset (rows cascade, files don't) — clear the storage dir/volume manually if it matters. (The former `V5__demo_data_reset.sql` was folded away by the schema squash below — and its existence is why the "add a migration" phrasing that stood here was once correct: when it was written, the only installation in existence was a production instance with no users to preserve.)

## Schema baseline — migrations squashed (2026-08-07)

The former `V1`..`V12` Flyway chain was **consolidated into a single `V1__init_schema.sql` baseline** (done while the product had zero real users). The new `V1` is the exact final schema the old chain produced — verified by `pg_dump` parity (byte-identical DDL) + seed-row-count match + a `contextLoads` boot on a fresh DB (Flyway migrate → Hibernate `validate` → context up). New schema changes continue from `V2` onward.

**Because a squash changes `V1`'s checksum and drops `V2..V12`, every existing DB must be recreated** — Flyway would otherwise fail checksum/missing-migration validation. Required one-time action per environment: **drop and recreate the database** (local `hamstrack` DB; **prod: drop the `postgres` data volume** so Flyway applies the baseline fresh — attachment blobs in `attachments_data`/S3 become orphaned, clear if needed). This was only safe because there was no production data to preserve.

Notable finding baked into the baseline: `issues.type_id` / `issues.status_id` carried **no FK** (the original V1 FKs were dropped by V6's `DROP TABLE … CASCADE` and never recreated; integrity was app-layer via `ProjectConfigService`). **Fixed in 0.17.0 by `V19__issues_taxonomy_fk.sql` (HD-13).** Two notes in applied migrations are therefore **superseded and cannot be edited** — Flyway checksums the whole script including comments, so a text-only edit to `V1` or `V9` breaks startup on every existing installation: `V1__init_schema.sql:355` ("type_id/status_id intentionally carry NO foreign key … it would make admin delete-with-remap fail on dangling refs") and `V9__components.sql:79` ("Deliberate contrast with issues.type_id/status_id, which carry NO FK"). Both premises about *delete-with-remap* were wrong: `AdminCatalogService` issues its bulk remap **before** the `DELETE` in the same transaction, so nothing is stranded. `V19`'s header carries the correction, and states the distinction that does survive — these two FKs are **single-column and buy no tenancy**, unlike the composite `issues_component_fk`/`issues_sprint_fk`. The historical migration-number references in the Gotchas (V2 CHAR fix, V9 `issue_seq` resync, etc.) are now historical; the entity-mapping lessons still apply and the baseline already reflects them.

**Post-squash chain (V2–V6) — number-collision caution:** after the V1 baseline, new migrations resumed: `V2__issue_hierarchy`, `V3__system_fields`, `V4__search_indexes`, `V5__saved_filters`, `V6__custom_field_search_indexes`. ## Advanced search & HQL (HD-21 core, HD-52 custom fields)

Global search is a **fully implemented** feature — NOT a stub (older docs/api-*.md wording that calls it a stub is stale). Package `com.hamstrack.search`.

**Endpoints** — `SearchController` at `/api/workspaces/{wsId}/search`:
- `POST /` — run HQL. `SearchRequest {query (≤2000 chars, ""=all), page, size (default 50, clamped 1..100)}` → `PageResponse<SearchResultRow>` (`SearchResultRow {IssueResponse issue, projectId, projectKey, projectName}`, reuses the board/backlog `IssueResponse`). 422 on parse error (carries highlight `position`) or semantic error (carries `field`).
- `GET /schema` — `SearchSchemaResponse {fields, keywords, values, insights}` for autocomplete; `values` embeds one small picklist per `valueSuggest` token a live field declares (each built from what the caller's visible projects reach, each capped) plus per-custom-field `CUSTOM:<key>` picklists. Members are deliberately not embedded — see `/suggest`.
- `GET /suggest?field=&q=` — bounded (≤20) typeahead for the fields whose value set is too large, or too caller-specific, for a picklist to carry whole (`SearchService.systemSuggestions` is the list). `SuggestResponse {field, suggestions[]}`, echoing the field name as the caller wrote it.

**Query language** (`parser/HqlParser` recursive descent over `parser/Lexer`): boolean `NOT > AND > OR` (left-assoc), parens, optional trailing `ORDER BY key [ASC|DESC], …`. Empty = all. Predicates `field OP value` / `field IN (…)` / `field IS [NOT] EMPTY`; operators `= != > < >= <= ~` (`~` = text match). Values must be quoted strings, numbers, or functions (`currentUser()`, `now()`, `startOfWeek()`) — a bareword raises a targeted "must be quoted" 422. DoS limits (constants in `HqlParser`): query ≤2000 chars, nesting ≤32, IN-list ≤200, ≤5 sort keys, ≤50 leaf predicates. System fields are whatever `FieldRegistry`'s constructor registers — read it rather than a list here, which goes stale one field before anyone notices. A name may also be registered as a **not-yet-queryable stub** (422 "not yet queryable", hidden from `/schema`); registration is what reserves a name, availability only says when it starts answering. `priority` supports ordered comparison via catalog `position` (inverted so `priority > "Low"` = more urgent; `ORDER BY priority DESC` = most-urgent-first). `text ~ term` = `LOWER(title|description) LIKE %term%` (escaped). `created`/`updated` = inclusive-day UTC windows; `due` = real DATE.

**Custom fields (HD-52):** any non-archived `field_def` reachable via a visible project's effective field set becomes queryable by its `key` — **a system field always wins** on name clash. Each predicate compiles to a correlated `EXISTS` over `IssueFieldValue` filtered by `field_id`, with per-type JSONB Criteria functions (`jsonb_scalar_text|numeric|date`, `jsonb_exists`/`?` for MULTI_SELECT membership) registered via a `JsonbFunctionContributor`. `!=` on a scalar = `NOT EXISTS(=)` (absent counts as not-equal); `IS EMPTY` = no value row. SELECT/MULTI_SELECT accept option label or id. Custom fields **non-sortable** in MVP (ORDER BY → 422). Backing indexes: V4 (composite `issues` indexes leading `workspace_id`), V6 (GIN on `issue_field_values.value`).

**The `project` field (HD-101, release 0.16.0).** Spec: `docs/design/hql-project-field-proposal.md`. Search is workspace-scoped by construction, so until this shipped there was no way to narrow a search to one project — and no way to disambiguate the fields that resolve names *across* the visible projects (two projects may each ship a "2.4.0" or run a "Sprint 1"). `project` supports `=`, `!=` and `IN` (negated as `NOT … IN (…)`, the grammar's only form — there is no `field NOT IN (…)` production for *any* field), sorts on `project.key`, and is **not nullable**: every issue has a project, so `project IS EMPTY` is a 422 rather than a silent empty result.

- **A key resolves; a name does not.** `UNIQUE(workspace_id, key)` makes a key an identity, while `projects.name` carries no uniqueness constraint and none is checked on create — a name operand would denote a *set*, which is the ambiguity the field exists to remove. The human name reaches the user where being wrong is cheap: the `PROJECT` picklist and `/suggest` publish `label = "Hamstrack (HD)"`, `value = "HD"` (the SPA inserts `value ?? label` and filters on both), and a miss that matches a visible project's name answers `No project with key 'Hamstrack' that you can search. Did you mean "HD" (Hamstrack)?`.
- **Tenancy.** The key map is built from `SearchScope.visibleProjectIds` and nothing wider, so an unknown, invisible or archived key is a field-anchored 422 — never a 404, never an empty 200. The refusal names the *category* rule ("Archived projects are not searchable") rather than the operand's fate, because telling archived from unknown needs a lookup over projects the scope hides — the seam that would become an existence oracle the day read visibility is permission-gated. It prescribes nothing: `project.archive` is outside the workspace curator set, so "unarchive it" would be a remedy most readers cannot perform.
- **The term never touches `SearchScope`.** It compiles as an ordinary predicate *inside* the scope conjunction (`project != "HD"` = "in a visible project other than HD"). Folding it into `visibleProjectIds` would look like an optimisation and would make the tenant boundary a function of query text.
- **No new query anywhere.** `/search`, `/schema` (≤200) and `/suggest?field=project` (≤20, prefix-matched on key *and* name, key order) are served from `ResolutionContext`, which already hydrates those projects to read their delivery capabilities. Pinned by `ProjectSearchQueryCountTest`. Not capability-narrowed — every project has a project. **DC and Cloud are identical, permanently**: a term present in one mode only would make saved-filter text non-portable across an export/import.
- ⚠ **Registering the name changes its meaning for any workspace that already keyed a *custom field* `project`** — a registered name outranks a tenant's own field (`FieldResolver`). The loud half is that stored values stop resolving (422); the **silent** half is that `/schema` drops a registry-claimed key, so that tenant's field vanishes from search vocabulary with no message while working everywhere else. `AdminFieldService.requireUnreservedKey` now refuses to *create* a field under a registered key (409, checked **after** slugification — a field called "Project" auto-slugs to `project`), which stops recurrence but is not retroactive. Detection query before any release that registers a name: `SELECT id, key, name, scope_workspace_id FROM field_defs WHERE lower(key) = '<new name>' AND archived_at IS NULL;` — a **global** def (`scope_workspace_id IS NULL`) makes the blast radius the whole instance.

**Tenancy (critical):** `SearchScope` is a Criteria predicate builder — `workspace = :ws AND project.id IN :visibleProjectIds` (the workspace's **non-archived** projects; empty set → match-nothing, never "all"), ANDed as the **outermost** conjunction on both page and count queries. Membership gate (`resolveWorkspace`) returns **404** whether the workspace is missing or the caller isn't a member (never 403). Name/member/custom-field resolution derives from the same visible-project set, so nothing resolves through a hidden project; all user input is bound parameters (injection-safe).

**Frontend:** `pages/SearchResultsPage.tsx` (route `/w/:wsId/search`), `components/TopSearchBar.tsx` (HQL box + `/schema` autocomplete; hidden without workspace context), `components/SavedFiltersPanel.tsx`. **Config:** `app.search.requests-per-minute` (`SEARCH_REQUESTS_PER_MINUTE`, default 120) — a per-principal budget over the whole `…/workspaces/*/search/**` path (`POST …/search`, `/schema`, `/suggest` and the Insights panel) **and `…/workspaces/*/filters/**`** (every saved-filter operation — HQL validation is search-surface work wherever it is mounted, and validating a filter builds the same `ResolutionContext` `/schema` pays for), 429 + `Retry-After` past it, master switch `app.rate-limit.enabled`. The Insights panel is inside this budget **and** the reports one (`ReportRateLimitConfig.INSIGHTS_PATH`), so it spends both and **the lower configured value binds** — it sits on the reports limiter deliberately, because removing that binding would raise the panel’s allowance to the search budget as a side effect. Otherwise always-on and gated purely by membership; **no DC/Cloud difference** (identical in both, no profile override).

## Delegated admin — scoped catalog (supersedes M1's "reserved for future")

> **Its authorization half is superseded by HD-123** (see *Roles & permissions* below), and the paragraphs here are kept as history. `scope/ScopeResolver`, `WorkspaceRole` and `ProjectRole` were **deleted in S3**: `requireWorkspaceAdmin` is now `workspace.taxonomy.manage`, `requireProjectAdmin` is `project.taxonomy.manage`, and the curator bypass is `project.curate.all` — all checked through `WorkspaceAccessService` + `ctx.permissions().require(...)`. `/projects/{pId}/admin/**` now answers **403** (not 404) to a workspace member who is not a project member. The scoping/visibility model below (`ScopeContext`, `stamp`/`owns`/`canSee`, three-scope catalog) is unchanged and still current.

**Built and in prod.** The catalog is now three-scoped; `scope_workspace_id`/`scope_project_id` are LIVE (mutually exclusive by DB CHECK in the V1 baseline). `issue/entity/Scoped.java` is implemented by Status, Priority, IssueType, FieldDef, Workflow, PrioritySet, FieldSet, IssueTypeSet.

- **GLOBAL** (both null) — the **system admin** (`hasRole("ADMIN")`).
- **WORKSPACE** (`scopeWorkspaceId` set) — the workspace's **OWNER/ADMIN**.
- **PROJECT** (`scopeProjectId` set) — the project's **MANAGER**.

**Roles:** `workspace/entity/WorkspaceRole` = **OWNER, ADMIN, MEMBER** (workspace-level, distinct from the system role). `scope/ScopeResolver`: `requireWorkspaceAdmin` (membership → 404 else; then ≥ADMIN → 403 else, `InsufficientWorkspaceRoleException`); `requireProjectAdmin` (workspace + project membership → 404 else; then ≥MANAGER → 403). **Tenancy rule (explicit):** non-membership and non-existence both 404; a member lacking the role (already knows it exists) gets 403. `scope/ScopeContext {workspaceId, projectId, ancestorWorkspaceId}`: `stamp(entity)` marks a new row at the current scope; `owns(entity)` = exact-scope (edit/delete gate); `canSee(entity)` = global ∪ ancestor-workspace ∪ own-project (visibility / usage-aggregation gate — keeps usage counts from spanning tenants).

**Endpoints:**
- Global: existing `/api/admin/**` (`AdminCatalogController`, `AdminWorkflowController`, `AdminFieldController`, `AdminProjectController`, `AdminUserController`), `ScopeContext.global()`.
- Workspace: `WorkspaceAdminController` at `/api/workspaces/{wsId}/admin/**` — full catalog CRUD (+`/archive`,`/unarchive`, `DELETE ?replaceWithId=`), workflows, priority-sets, issue-type-sets, fields (`DELETE ?dropValues=`), field-sets, `/{id}/usage`, and the binding matrix (`GET /projects`, `GET /binding-options`, `PATCH /projects/{projectId}/bindings`). Each handler: `requireWorkspaceAdmin` + `ScopeContext.workspace(wsId)`.

## Issue hierarchy (V2)

## Delivery capabilities — Kanban / Scrum / Releases (HD-95, V12)

The model that answers "what does the app do differently depending on how a team delivers?". Spec: `docs/design/delivery-paths-proposal.md`. Implemented as seven slices, HD-102…HD-108.

**Not a three-way choice.** A project stores three independent capabilities — `board` (`KANBAN|SCRUM`, the existing `board_mode` column), `releases`, `estimation` — and the server derives a display label (`KANBAN` / `SCRUM` / `RELEASES` / `CUSTOM`). Releases are orthogonal to the board: a team can run Scrum *and* ship versions, which a three-way enum makes unrepresentable, and which is also the only shape that can classify projects that already exist on upgrade. The UI still presents three named cards.

Three rules do the work, and every future surface is checked against them:

- **A — capabilities gate the UI, never the API.** No status code anywhere depends on a capability. A project with `releases` off still accepts and returns version data; a hidden control is not a permission. Enforced by construction: the capability accessors are read in exactly two places, both DTO mappers.
- **B — controls are gated, values never are.** With a capability off, existing values still render read-only, with a visible reason. This is what makes switching provably non-destructive.
- **C — the bootstrap invariant.** Every capability has an enabling affordance visible *while it is off*, and first use turns it on with a reversible notice. Rule C exists because of a real production bug: V11 defaulted every pre-existing project to Kanban and the Backlog hid both sprint-creation entry points until a sprint existed, so there was no way to create a first sprint at all. C makes that class of dead end impossible rather than patching one screen.

Other decisions worth knowing:

- **Two opposite default policies, deliberately.** New projects start lean (Kanban, releases and estimation off); every project existing at V12 keeps everything it had, and becomes `SCRUM` if it already owned a sprint — 163 real projects took that path. An upgrade must never take away what a team already had.
- **`preset` is derived and rejected on write** (400), so a client cannot echo back the `delivery` object it read from a GET — it must strip `preset` first.
- **`boardMode` remains as a deprecated mirror**; sending it with a conflicting `delivery.board` is a 400.
- **Search: suggestions narrow, resolution never does.** `/search/schema` and `/search/suggest` omit fields and picklist names for capabilities that are off, but a hidden field still compiles and runs — a saved filter must not break because somebody flipped a toggle. Consequence for integrators: the *suggested* set is no longer the *resolvable* set.
- **Retired-key alias**: `story_points` resolves to the native `storyPoints`, placed **after** custom-field lookup so it can never shadow a tenant's own field of that key (`RetiredFieldAliases`, deliberately not a `FieldRegistry` entry).

## Roles & permissions (epic HD-123, V13–V16)

**Authorization is one resolution and one primitive.** Specs: `docs/design/roles-permissions-proposal.md` (the epic — read **§20 Corrections** with it; three of its statements were wrong), `roles-permissions-s4-spec.md` (custom roles), `roles-permissions-s7-spec.md` (access mode). The user-facing contract is `docs/api-cloud.md` / `docs/api-dc.md` → *Roles*, *Permissions*, *Custom roles*, *Project access*. Eight slices: **S1** HD-124 (model, landed dark) · **S2** HD-125 (project call sites) · **S3** HD-126 (delete the old layer) · **S4** HD-127 (custom-roles API) · **S5** HD-128 (SPA renders from permissions) · **S6** HD-129 (People & Roles screens) · **S7** HD-130 (access mode + default-role pickers) · **S8** HD-131 (docs) — plus HD-132 (workspace member role change/removal) and HD-136 (the stranding guard).

**The catalog is code — there is no `permissions` table.** 29 permissions (9 workspace-scoped, 20 project-scoped) live in `common.security.Permission` and nowhere else: a permission without a call site is meaningless and call sites are code, so a table could only ever drift from the enum. What the database stores is *grants* — `role_permissions` rows keyed by the wire string (`VARCHAR(64)`, never a PG ENUM), validated on read by `PermissionConverter`. **Adding a permission is an enum constant + one `require(...)` line + one seed row per built-in that should hold it — never a migration.** Keys are `area.action`, lowercase, and are permanent wire format; the enum validates their shape at class-init because the own-only wire form is `key + ":own"`, and a `:` inside a key would make every client's `split` resolve an own-only grant in the widening direction. Unknown keys are **422 on the write path and log-and-drop on the read path**: `PermissionConverter` runs inside `requireMember` on every authenticated request naming a workspace, and dropping a grant can only narrow a role while throwing would take down the instance.

**A role is a named bundle of permissions, and nothing else** — no ordinal, no ladder, no `isAtLeast`. `roles` (V13) holds the built-in templates (`workspace_id IS NULL`, with `built_in = (workspace_id IS NULL)` as a CHECK) plus each workspace's own, under `UNIQUE NULLS NOT DISTINCT (workspace_id, scope, key)`; `role_permissions` holds `(role_id, permission, own_only)`. **Eight built-ins with fixed literal UUIDs** (`workspace/entity/BuiltInRoles`, mirroring the migration so the resolver can name one with zero queries): workspace **Owner / Admin / Member**, project **Project admin (`MANAGER`) / Team lead (`TEAM_LEAD`, V16) / Contributor (`MEMBER`) / Commenter / Viewer**. The keys are the legacy enum names on purpose — that is what kept `myRole` wire-compatible — but nothing ranks them any more: the ordinal enums `WorkspaceRole`/`ProjectRole` and `scope/ScopeResolver` were **deleted in S3**, and V15 dropped the three legacy `role` VARCHAR columns they were stored in. V14 is the additive half (`role_id` on `workspace_members`/`project_members`/`workspace_invites`, `default_project_role_id` on `workspaces` and `projects`, `workspaces.project_access_mode`) and carries the no-op backfill; V15 is destructive, which is why they are separate files (see `docs/release-checklist.md`).

**A grant is `(permission, own_only)`.** `has(ISSUE_EDIT)` asks "may you edit *anyone's*?" and an own-only grant answers **false**; `has(ISSUE_EDIT, isOwn)` is the ownership-aware form, so nothing can accidentally satisfy the unrestricted question with an own-only grant. `comment.edit` is own-**required** (editing another person's words is not a capability this product ships at any role); `issue.edit`, `issue.delete`, `comment.delete`, `attachment.delete` and `label.manage` are own-*optional*. The wire form is the flat `["issue.edit", "comment.edit:own"]`, in catalog order.

**One resolution, one primitive, constant cost.** `WorkspaceAccessService` — the single tenancy *and* authorization chokepoint, whose `requireProjectMember` was renamed **`resolveProject`** in S1 because it never read `project_members` — resolves an immutable `PermissionSet` **once per request** onto `WorkspaceContext` / `ProjectContext` / `IssueContext`. A call site then authorizes with one line:

```java
ctx.permissions().require(Permission.SPRINT_MANAGE);   // 403 MissingPermissionException; detail names the key
```

Cost (`PermissionResolutionQueryCountTest` asserts all three): **2** statements for a workspace-scoped resolution, **4** for a project-scoped one, **0** to check a permission — a handler that checks six permissions costs exactly what one that checks none costs. That holds because built-in ids are compile-time constants, the membership finders **`JOIN FETCH m.role`** (drop the fetch and the lazy `@ManyToOne` adds a SELECT to every authenticated request), and `RolePermissionCache` holds the permission sets. Two rules follow: never re-query `workspace_members`/`project_members` for an authorization decision — the answer is already on the context; and resolve request-supplied role ids only through `RoleRepository.findAssignable(id, workspaceId, scope)` (`RoleRepository` deliberately does not extend `JpaRepository`, so a bare `findById` does not compile), because a flat `PermissionSet` has no memory of where a grant came from and a WORKSPACE role accepted into `project_members.role_id` would put `workspace.edit` into that member's project permissions.

**Tenancy first, permission second, always.** Non-existence and non-membership are both **404**; `MissingPermissionException` (403, detail `"Requires permission: sprint.manage"`) is reachable only by a proven member, by construction — a `PermissionSet` exists only once resolution has proved membership. Reads are deliberately **not** permission-gated in v1 (there is no `issue.view`/`project.view`); when visibility lands (epic §8.5) its failure mode must be 404, never 403.

**Built-in seeds are as load-bearing as call sites.** Workspace **Owner/Admin hold `project.curate.all`** — exactly `{project.edit, component.manage, version.manage, sprint.manage}`, today's curator bypass transcribed and nothing more — and deliberately **not `project.administer.all`** (all twenty project permissions; held by no built-in, grantable to a custom role, mintable only by an Owner). Workspace **Member holds `label.manage:own`**, because label editing short-circuits for a label's creator. `BuiltInRoleSeedParityTest` and `RoleIdsMatchMigrationTest` pin **exact key sets**, not counts — a count cannot see a *swap*. Note that `PermissionParityTest`, which proved each S2 call-site conversion behaviour-preserving against the old ladders, was **deleted with those ladders in S3** (it had no comparand left); the seed-parity tests are the guard now, so a doc or comment that still points at it is out of date.

**Custom roles (S4, HD-127).** A workspace defines roles of its own on top of the eight built-ins, composed from the same keys and occupying no rung.

- **Creation is duplication-only** — there is no `POST /roles`, only `POST …/roles/{sourceRoleId}/duplicate`. The reason is that the grant ceiling is a **subset** rule: a role assembled from an empty checklist (`project.member.manage` and nothing else) can assign *nobody*, because it is a superset of none of the built-ins, and discovering that as a 403 six weeks later is the failure the model exists to prevent. Duplication always starts from a superset; "from scratch" is duplicating **Viewer**, which grants nothing, and is then a deliberate act. `scope` and the permission set come from the source; `key` is generated server-side from the name and never accepted from a client (which is also what stops a custom role reusing a built-in key); `name` is what uniqueness guards, because names are what users read.
- **The grant ceiling — everything you hand out, you hold.** It compares permission *sets*, never role names, and it compares them per grant width (an unrestricted grant is not covered by an own-only one). It bounds **both ends** of a write — the role as it stands today as well as the set being sent — so an administrator cannot *strip*, delete, or even *rename* a workspace-scoped role that grants more than they hold (a relabelled role is the next administrator handing out something they believe they understand). The refusal is a 403 naming the first uncovered key.
- **The workspace Owner is exempt inside their own workspace**, for defining and for assigning — they are the root of trust, and the exemption is **the only reason `project.administer.all` is mintable at all**: no built-in role holds it, so without the exemption the product would ship a permission nobody could ever grant. An **Admin is not exempt**, which is the point of the distinction. The *definition* ceiling applies to WORKSPACE-scoped roles only (a project role has no comparand at workspace level, and inventing one would forbid duplicating Contributor — the product's primary recipe); PROJECT-scoped roles are guarded instead by `SELF_HELD_ROLE`: you may not widen a project role you hold yourself (409).
- **Delete with reassign.** `DELETE …/roles/{id}?reassignToRoleId=`; a role still in use without one is a 409 `ROLE_IN_USE` carrying the **whole usage object** (members, invites, project members, projects, `defaultForProjects`, `defaultForWorkspace`) so a client renders the remap dialog straight from the refusal. Reassignment is a grant to every holder at once, so the ceiling bounds it too, and both `default_project_role_id` columns count as usage.
- `PATCH` takes a **full-replacement** `permissions` array plus an optimistic-locking `version`; scope is immutable (editing it would turn every existing assignment into a wrong-scope row) and a permission's scope must equal the role's (422). Built-ins are read-only — 409, not 403, because the caller's permissions are fine and what refuses is the nature of the resource. Per-workspace cap `app.roles.max-custom-per-workspace` (default 50), exact rather than advisory because the count is taken under a row lock on the workspace; over it is 409 `ROLE_LIMIT_REACHED`.

**Project access mode (S7, HD-130).** `workspaces.project_access_mode` is `OPEN` | `STRICT`, and it decides exactly one thing: **whether somebody who was never added to a project inherits anything there.** The chain is: the explicit `project_members` row → the project's `default_project_role_id` → the workspace's `default_project_role_id` → the built-in **Contributor** — and *everything after the first link applies only while the workspace is `OPEN`*. In `STRICT`, a member with no explicit row holds the empty set. It is **not an enforcement switch**: permissions always mean what they say in both modes, there is no `if (enforcementEnabled)` branch anywhere, and `STRICT` narrows **writes** only — no read is lost (narrowing reads would be private projects by the back door). `OPEN` with a NULL default is what V14 sets everywhere, which is what makes the upgrade a provable no-op. Flipping writes no membership row and is reversible byte for byte; the declared defaults stay stored while `STRICT` (inert, not erased), which is why the default-role ceilings apply in **both** modes — a bound that only bit while `OPEN` is one you step over by flipping twice. Two consequences that surprise people: a workspace Owner is **not** a rescue (`project.curate.all` contains no issue or comment permission, so in a `STRICT` project nobody has been added to, nobody can file an issue — Owner included; the preview counts exactly this as `projectsWithNoWriters`), and `WorkspaceAccessService.defaultProjectRole` (mode-aware — "what does somebody inherit") must never be swapped for `declaredDefaultProjectRole` (mode-independent — "what is configured", two legitimate callers, both the current end of a ceiling).

**The stranding guard (HD-136, extended by S7).** `project.member.manage` sits deliberately **outside** the curator bypass, so a project that loses its last administrator is unmanageable by *anyone* — including a workspace Owner, who holds no `project.member.manage` in a project they have no row in and therefore cannot add one member back. No endpoint can repair that state, so the invariant is enforced instead: *a project that has an administrator must keep one*, where "administrator" means an **ACTIVE member holding `project.member.manage`** — never one particular role id (the first cut looked for the built-in Project admin and so did not protect a project whose only administrator held a custom role; the role ids are resolved from the grant instead). `ProjectAdminGuard` is the one implementation, for **nine enumerated doors** — project member removal and demotion; workspace member removal, both for explicit rows and (S7) for administrators who held no row and administered purely by inheritance; a role edit or delete-with-reassign that demotes every holder at once; and the three S7 writes that change the mode or either default. **An unlisted door is the bug.** The workspace-removal refusal is retryable: repeat with `?adoptStrandedProjects=true` and the caller is granted the built-in **Team lead** role in each project that would otherwise strand (V16 explains at length why a membership-only role would be a one-way demotion rather than a smaller grant). Inheritance-only stranding (`STRANDED_BY_INHERITANCE`) has **no** retry — adoption would narrow the rescuer.

**Operational properties an operator or the next developer will meet.**

- **The `roleView` cache TTL is 10 s, and it is a security window, not config freshness** (`CacheConfig.ROLE_VIEW_TTL`, `sync = true`, evicted after commit on the node that served the edit). A permission **revocation is honoured for up to 10 s on every other instance**, plus the tail of a request that had already resolved its context (snapshot semantics: a role change committed mid-request is not observed by that request). Do not raise it and do not fold it back into the shared 60 s cache spec; there is deliberately **no property for it**, because a configurable TTL on an authorization cache is a per-deployment way to lengthen that window. The documented upgrade path is PostgreSQL `LISTEN`/`NOTIFY`, deferred on cost. `RolePermissionCache` is a separate bean because `@Cacheable` is proxy-applied and a self-invocation would silently bypass it.
- **Membership is not cached, and neither is the access mode.** A role *re*assignment — moving a person to a different role, and the whole of `DELETE /roles/{id}?reassignToRoleId=` — bites on that person's very next request on every node; only a change to a role's *contents* has a window. Permissions are **never** put in the JWT: a token is not revocable and a demotion has to take effect immediately.
- **`app.locking.lock-timeout-ms`** (`DB_LOCK_TIMEOUT_MS`, default 3000, range 100–60000) bounds how long a transaction waits for a row lock. **Since HD-151 it is applied to every transaction** by `BoundedJpaTransactionManager`, in the same round trip as the statement bound, and `LockTimeout`'s seven call sites survive as an explicit re-assert on the paths that lock deliberately. Both had to move together: `statement_timeout` counts lock-wait time, so a statement bound shipped without a global lock bound turns every contended ordinary write into a **422 that forbids a retry which would have worked** — the ticket's own defect class, inside the ticket. It is applied with `SET LOCAL` inside each transaction the app opens, never on the datasource (Flyway shares it and a migration legitimately waits). Exceeding it is a retryable **409 with `Retry-After`**, never a 500. PostgreSQL reads `0` as "wait for ever" — the exact state this exists to prevent — so `@Min` refuses it at startup, and the field is a primitive `int` on purpose so a blank `DB_LOCK_TIMEOUT_MS=` fails the boot loudly instead of silently reverting to the default. **Capped in practice from 0.17.0**: `app.persistence.statement-timeout-ms` must stay at least 2x this value, so at its default of 10000 the lock bound may not exceed 5000 — the constraint binds from both ends and `DatabaseTimeoutConsistency` refuses the boot either way.
- **`app.persistence.statement-timeout-ms`** (`DB_STATEMENT_TIMEOUT_MS`, default 10000, range 1000-600000) bounds how long **any one statement** may run, and is applied by `BoundedJpaTransactionManager` — a `JpaTransactionManager` subclass that issues `SET LOCAL statement_timeout` in `doBegin` — to **every** transaction the application opens. Deliberately not per call site: a lock wait is structural and can be found by reading the code, a slow statement is volumetric and cannot, so an opt-in list would describe our measurement history rather than the risk. The transaction manager is also already the line between application work and Flyway's, so **migrations are unbounded by construction** (a `connection-init-sql` would catch them, which is why it is rejected). What is enumerated instead is the complement — `StatementTimeout.exemptCurrentTransaction(reason)` — and that set is **empty in application code**: demo seeding needs nothing (a long transaction of short statements) and there is no admin export. Exceeding it is **422 `STATEMENT_BUDGET_EXCEEDED` with no `Retry-After`** (an identical retry costs identical time), plus a WARN naming the knob and a `hamstrack.db.statement_budget_exceeded{method,route}` counter. Must be >= 2x the lock bound or the boot fails (`DatabaseTimeoutConsistency`): `statement_timeout` counts lock-wait time, so a smaller value fires first and silently replaces the retryable 409. Two implementation traps, both found by tests rather than by reading: the bean must be **named `transactionManager`** (Spring Data JPA resolves it by name, and a misnamed one fails every repository call at request time, not at boot), and the refusal must also be declared for `org.hibernate.QueryTimeoutException` (the JPA spec makes Hibernate throw a bare `PersistenceException` once the transaction is marked for rollback, so the direct-`EntityManager` path — Insights — 500s otherwise).
- **`app.roles.max-custom-per-workspace`** (`ROLES_MAX_CUSTOM_PER_WORKSPACE`, default 50) and **`app.workspace.default-project-access-mode`** (`DEFAULT_PROJECT_ACCESS_MODE`, default `OPEN`, applies to **newly created** workspaces only and never migrates an existing one) are the other two knobs. Both are identical in `dc` and `cloud` with no profile override: custom roles and access modes are product features, not plan features, and if they were ever limited it would be by lowering a number — never a second code path. All three are documented in the DC operator-settings table in `docs/api-dc.md`.

**Frontend (S5/S6/S7).** `hooks/usePermissions.ts` is the **single place the SPA is allowed to answer "may I?"**: it reads `myPermissions` off the workspace/project responses the app already fetches and compares against the literal key the server checks. **No component may read `myRole` for a decision** — that is what produced HD-98 and HD-116, the same client-side copy of a server predicate drifting from it, twice, and `myRole === 'MANAGER'` cannot express a custom role at all. A missing, failed or unloaded permission set **denies**; `isLoading` distinguishes "not yet" from "no" so a control can render *disabled* rather than vanish; `:own` is a suffix, not a prefix. Screens: `pages/settings/WorkspacePeoplePage`, `WorkspaceRolesPage` (+ the role editor), `ProjectPeoplePage` — with the always-visible **Default access** card, this feature's Rule C affordance — and the project-access switch on `WorkspaceGeneralPage`.

**`myPermissions` is advisory, for rendering only.** The API is the enforcement boundary: a client that hides nothing is still safe, and a gate that is wrong shows a button that 403s rather than a door that silently opens. The same rule governs S7's impact preview and the `settable` block — **counts are advisory, refusals are authoritative**: `strandedProjects` and every ceiling check are re-derived inside the write's own transaction whether or not anyone previewed.

## Reports (epic HD-5, V17–V18)

**Seven read-only surfaces, one provenance contract, and a list of things they refuse to do.** Spec: `docs/design/reports-proposal.md`. The user-facing contract is `docs/api-cloud.md` / `docs/api-dc.md` → *Reports* and `openapi.yaml`. Eight slices: **R1** HD-28 (flow) · **R2** HD-137 (the sprint scope ledger, landed dark) · **R3** HD-138 (cycle/lead time, aging WIP) · **R4** HD-29 (sprint burn-up, sprint review) · **R5** HD-139 (velocity) · **R6** HD-140 (Insights) · **R7** HD-141 (CSV exports) · **R8** HD-142 (docs).

**Six are project-scoped** under `/api/workspaces/{wsId}/projects/{pId}/reports` — `flow`, `cycle-time`, `aging`, `sprint-burnup`, `sprint-review`, `velocity` — **each with a `.csv` sibling** (append `.csv`). The seventh, **Insights**, is `POST …/search/insights`: it is workspace-scoped and lives on the search path because its dataset is an HQL query rather than a project. Packages `com.hamstrack.report.{controller,service,repository,dto,csv,ratelimit}`.

**Gated by project membership and nothing else.** There is deliberately no `report.view` permission: this product has no read permissions, and every number a report prints is derivable by the same member from the search API they already hold, so a gate here would protect nothing while being the first read gate in the codebase. The recorded trigger for revisiting is the day a per-assignee breakdown or a cross-project rollup is proposed — see the refusals below.

### The sprint scope ledger (V18) — why it exists

`sprint_scope_events` is an append-only log of membership changes: one row per `(sprint, issue, ADDED|REMOVED)` with `occurred_at`, the actor, and **snapshots of the issue key and story points**. R2 built it dark, one slice ahead of its first reader, because the alternative is unbuildable — you cannot reconstruct "what was in the sprint on the 14th" from current state, and a burn-up drawn from current state is the reason burndowns get distrusted.

Three properties are load-bearing and each cost a review round:

- **The issue FK is `ON DELETE SET NULL`, never `CASCADE`** — so deleting an issue cannot quietly rewrite a completed sprint's record. The consequence for every reader: **the join to `issues` must be `LEFT`.** An inner join silently drops exactly those rows, and the report becomes a smaller, plausible, wrong sprint with nothing to notice. Both sprint repositories say so in-file.
- **A scope change is a membership change only.** Re-estimating an issue writes no row. That single rule is what makes the burn-up trustworthy where a burndown is not: every step in the scope line has a timestamp, an issue and an author.
- **Membership has exactly one implementation.** `SprintLedger.LedgerIssue.memberAt` / `memberBefore` / `closedBefore` / `outcomeAt` are the only definitions of "in the sprint at time T", shared by the burn-up, the review and velocity. Two reports over one ledger that disagreed about "done in this sprint" would discredit both — and they did, at exactly one instant, until the rule moved onto the shared record.

Velocity reads up to twelve sprints' ledgers in **one** sweep (`ORDER BY sprint_id`) rather than calling the single-sprint reader N times; it assembles the same `LedgerIssue` records, so the cheaper query did not buy a second definition of membership.

### The provenance contract

Every report response carries the same `ReportMeta`: `computedAt`, `basedOnIssues`, `truncated`, `cap`, `firstIssueAt`, `unmatchedFilters`. It exists because the recurring complaint about reporting features is *"these numbers don't match what I expected"*, and the mechanism behind it is always a report that quietly left data out.

- **`basedOnIssues` is counted in the database, above the cap** — so when truncation bites it is deliberately *larger* than what came back, because its job is to say how many issues the report was *about*. R4 shipped it shrinking and had to be fixed; R5's statement was written the corrected way from the start.
- **`firstIssueAt` takes filters and never a window.** It is a property of the *project*, not of the sample. Each report computed it its own way once and the field silently meant three different things (project-wide on flow, the windowed completed sample on cycle time, currently-open issues on aging) — three populations, one name, every one of them a plausible date. Applying the window is also circular: the earliest issue inside a fortnight is at most a fortnight old, so a five-year-old project reported a fortnight of history and the thin-data banner fired on every project. Insights leaves it `null` rather than inventing a workspace-wide meaning for a per-project field.
- **Suppression is `null`s inside a present container, never a missing container.** Percentiles below five samples, a velocity band below three sprints. A statistic over too small a sample is arithmetically defined and informationally worthless, and printing noise is worse than printing nothing.
- **Two limits, two signals.** `meta.truncated` means the row cap (`app.reports.max-rows`) bit. The burn-up's day clip is `seriesTruncatedAt`, and Insights' bucket caps are `slicesTruncated` / `cellsTruncated` — folding either into `truncated` once put "20 000" in a banner about a report that had dropped nothing.

### The two point-reading rules

All three sprint reports read the same ledger and deliberately do not read the same estimate. **Do not "fix" one to match the others** — each is wrong for the others' question:

| Report | Points | Because |
|---|---|---|
| burn-up | the issue's **current** estimate | it charts a sprint still running; a re-estimate moves the line, including its past |
| review | the ledger's **entry snapshot** | the retro question is what a thing weighed *when it entered* |
| velocity | the ledger's **entry snapshot** | every sprint it describes is frozen; current points would move a bar drawn four months ago *and the band computed from it* |

The one exception is inherited rather than chosen: a **deleted** issue has no current estimate, so the burn-up weighs it by its snapshot too — the only number left.

### What the epic refused, and on what evidence

These are policy, not backlog, and they are enforced structurally rather than annotated:

- **No per-person breakdown of a published metric.** Velocity compared between people or teams is read as a productivity score, and teams respond by inflating estimates — the number rises and the delivery does not. `VelocityReportRepository` does not select `actor_id` or `assignee_id` at all, so there is nothing to break down *by* without first re-opening the statement; `VelocityRefusalTest` pins the DTO, the SQL and the CSV column list.
- **No aggregate above the project.** There is no workspace-level velocity and there must not be one: comparing two teams means opening two pages and doing the arithmetic by hand, and *that friction is the design*. A rollup added "for convenience" deletes the mitigation and keeps the metric.
- **No single quotable number.** The forecast is a p50/p85 with its sample size attached — there is no `averageVelocity` field — precisely so there is nothing to paste into a status report. Percentiles, never a mean: one washed-out sprint drags a mean somewhere no sprint ever was.
- **The line is a published metric versus an ad-hoc query.** Insights offers `ASSIGNEE` as a grouping dimension while velocity refuses it, and that is the same policy rather than an exception: velocity has a stable URL and is read out in a ceremony, Insights is whatever HQL somebody just typed and is not addressable as "the team's number". That distinction is the one to defend if a later slice proposes an assignee *report*.
- **No projection.** The burn-up line ends where it ends; forecasting is velocity's job, with a stated sample size.
- **No configurable gadget dashboard.** Insights is the replacement: one panel whose dataset is the query already in the search box, so there is nothing to double-count across widgets and a saved filter becomes a saved report at no cost.

### Insights (R6) — the shape that is different

`POST …/search/insights` takes a `{query, measure, slice, segment}` body and returns a small pivot. Three deliberate divergences from the other six: it is a **POST** (the query is a 2 000-char body, so no HTTP cache will ever serve it — the 60-second de-duplication is client-side); its refusals are **422 naming the parameter**, not 400, because the tokens arrive in a JSON body and are resolved in the service, which is what every other refusal under `/search` looks like; and it is **workspace-scoped**. `GET /search/schema` grew an `insights` block listing the capability-narrowed measures and dimensions — suggestions narrow, resolution never does, exactly as with HQL fields.

**Bars and cells are capped separately and computed separately** (200 slices, 1000 cells, fixed constants). Deriving a bar by summing its cells would make every truncated response quietly understate its own chart. `bucket.hql` is a server-built click-through fragment and is **null where a correct one cannot be produced** — an ambiguous name across two visible projects would be *wider* than the bar it came from, and a completed sprint or archived label would 422 on click. A client must not synthesise its own.

### CSV exports (R7)

**One row per plotted data point — not a list of issues.** That is the feature: where an export like this exists at all it usually hands back a flat issue list, which is the documented disappointment. Above the rows sits a comment header carrying the report, project, window, measure, percentiles-or-why-suppressed, `computedAt`, `basedOnIssues` and truncation — which is what makes an exported file still self-describing weeks later.

**Every line in the file is CSV cells, the comment header included.** Both reviewers found the header formula-injectable: a raw comment line is still split on commas, so only its *first* cell begins with the hash, and a project or sprint name containing a comma opens a new cell free to begin with an equals sign. The whole line is now one quoted cell. Consequence: a reader configured to drop comment lines by a leading hash no longer skips the header — there is no format in which a line both starts with a bare hash and is a single cell. Text cells are apostrophe-guarded against formula injection; **numeric, date and enum cells deliberately are not**, because a negative `net` or `delta` legitimately starts with a minus and guarding it would turn a column into text that will not sum.

**Known gap:** the proposal's "hands off to the existing search export path" — that path does not exist. There is no CSV or export endpoint under `/search`, and it cannot be built client-side either: HQL has no `project` field (so a handoff cannot be scoped) and no `resolved`/`closed` field (so the flow report's resolved half is not expressible as a query). The SPA ships the "Download matching issues" button **listed and disabled with the reason on it**, because hiding it would leave the series CSV as the only export — which is precisely how a reader concludes it holds the issue list. Filed as its own ticket.

### Throttling, config and the rule behind them

The per-principal budgets this subsystem owns are in-memory per node and switched off by `app.rate-limit.enabled` (a count here would go stale one entry before the list does — HD-191 added two more on the write surface, HD-174 one more on the planning surface):

- **`app.reports.requests-per-minute`** (`REPORTS_REQUESTS_PER_MINUTE`, default 60) over `…/projects/*/reports/**` **plus** `…/search/insights` **plus** `…/workspaces/*/storage/projects` (HD-191), each bound explicitly because it is O(tenant content) work that does not live under that path — the storage breakdown is a grouped aggregate over every attachment row in the workspace, which is this budget's exact denomination.
- **`app.search.requests-per-minute`** (`SEARCH_REQUESTS_PER_MINUTE`, default 120) over `…/search/**` **and** `…/filters/**`. A separate pot because somebody typing in a search box legitimately fires several requests a minute and must not be starved to protect charts.
- **`app.planning.requests-per-minute`** (`PLANNING_REQUESTS_PER_MINUTE`, default 240) over `…/projects/*/backlog/**` — the planning view and every section read under it, on one pattern (HD-174). Earned not by response size but because the per-section stats query is **unconditional and cap-blind**; "bounded by `MAX_PLANNING_VIEW_ROWS`" is not "small", that bound being 20 000 assembled issues in one unpaged response, the same number as `app.reports.max-rows`. A third pot rather than the reports one because a card dragged *across* sections costs two section refreshes, so an ordinary grooming session would reach 60/min in three minutes and a 429 mid-drag reads as a product defect. **240 is derived** — ~70 planning requests/min from one very busy tab, ~140 with a second project open, so ~3.4× the first; the derivation is kept once, in `PlanningProperties`.

**Insights is inside both patterns and spends both; the lower configured value binds.** It sits on the reports limiter deliberately — removing that binding would raise the panel's allowance to the search budget as a side effect.

**The planning surface's real protection is the occupancy bound below, not its rate pot** — `BacklogService.view` is `@Transactional(readOnly = true)` over the whole method, so one pool connection is held across `12 + N` statements (32 at `AGILE_MAX_OPEN_SPRINTS=20`) and `DB_STATEMENT_TIMEOUT_MS` bounds each statement, not their sum: a worst-case hold of ~320 s against a 30 s `connectionTimeout` for everybody else. It joins the **existing** share rather than getting one of its own (**ADR-0031**), so HD-174 added no number that must sit below `DB_POOL_MAX_SIZE` and therefore no new way to fail a boot; a second ceiling would have turned `ExpensiveReadShare`'s derive-from-the-pool default into a *partition*, which on a pool of 4 is 1 permit per surface. The accepted cost is that planning, reports and search now refuse each other under saturation.

**And since HD-182 those registrations carry a second, different kind of control: an occupancy bound** (`common.ratelimit.PerPrincipalInFlightLimit` / `ExpensiveReadConcurrencyLimit`, `app.expensive-read.*`, env `EXPENSIVE_READ_*`, defaults 3 per principal / 6 per instance / 1000 ms wait). The budgets above bound how *often* a caller may ask; none of them bounded how many they may have *running*, and a rate spends the same unit whether a request takes 8 ms or 8 s — so its protection evaporates exactly as an instance slows down. A load probe measured the consequence: one principal, inside their documented allowance, saturated a replica and everything else on it failed on connection acquisition. The bound is a **counted share of the one Hikari pool, not a second `DataSource`** (ADR-0030): the rest of the API always retains `DB_POOL_MAX_SIZE − max-in-flight` connections. It is spent by the same `PrincipalThrottleInterceptor` on the same registrations (**one permit per request**, so insights takes one and not two), released in `afterCompletion`, and **never carried across the async gap** — `afterCompletion` does not run when a handler starts async processing, so `afterConcurrentHandlingStarted` releases and a category test forbids async handlers on this surface. Refusals are `429` + `Retry-After: 1` with `errorType` `TOO_MANY_IN_FLIGHT` (your own share) or `EXPENSIVE_SURFACE_BUSY` (the instance's, and that one may name no count, no remedy the reader cannot perform and no administrator). `PoolShareConsistency` fails the boot unless `per-principal ≤ max-in-flight < poolSize` and WARNs above 60 % of the pool — **against numbers an operator typed**: both ceilings ship as `-1` and `ExpensiveReadShare` derives them from the live Hikari maximum (60 % of it, capped at the shipped 6, per-principal clamped to fit), because a literal default crash-looped every upgrade of an install running `DB_POOL_MAX_SIZE=6` or less having configured nothing. `-1` is also a **supported operator value** — the way to ask for the derivation where the environment is a ConfigMap or a task definition and there is no line to comment out. The **mixed** configuration is narrowed rather than refused: a typed per-principal ceiling above a *derived* share is clamped down to it with a WARN naming both numbers and the pool (pool 4 pins `3` above a derived `2` — the exact line `.env.prod.example` shows), so the boot failure survives only where **both** numbers were typed, which is the one case an operator stated a relation. On a pool of 6 or smaller the two derived ceilings are **equal** by construction, so one caller can reach the whole share alone and only the reservation for the rest of the API still binds; that consequence is stated in `.env.prod.example` beside the clamp. Its off switch is **its own** (`EXPENSIVE_READ_LIMIT_ENABLED`), deliberately outside `RATE_LIMIT_ENABLED` — so the bound stays on across the test suite, where a leaked permit fails tests loudly instead of costing production capacity silently. **How long one permit may be held is bounded outside the properties**, because a permit is taken before the request body is read and returned after the response is written, i.e. the client paces part of its life. **The bound that makes that hold finite is `PerPrincipalInFlightLimit.sweepStalePermits`** (force-release past `statement_timeout` + 60 s, counted in `hamstrack_expensive_read_permit_force_released_total` and alerted by `ExpensiveReadPermitForceReleased`; the counter is incremented only when the sweep actually won the release race, since it is the sole discriminator between a leaked permit and a held one). Two layers raise the price of the attempt without ending it: `TomcatUploadTimeoutCustomizer` (`disableUploadTimeout=false` + 20 s `connectionUploadTimeout`, inside the app so every deployment has it) is a **tightening**, not a new bound — Tomcat's default leaves the body's read timeout at the connector's `connectionTimeout` (60 s, operator-settable), measured in the sources and by a runtime probe — and `read_body` in the bundled `Caddyfile` is an absolute deadline at our own edge only. The watchdog's over-issue is safe **because every handler on this surface is synchronous** (`ThrottleCoverageTest.noExpensiveReadHandlerIsAsynchronous`): a streaming one would be given a permit back while still holding a connection. Gauges: `hamstrack_expensive_read_in_flight` (per replica; `max()`, never `sum()`) — pinned at the ceiling it means a *leaked* permit only if the force-release counter is flat, and *held* permits if it is climbing.

The durable rule underneath: **what earns a throttle is the work a handler does, not where it is mounted.** Saved filters are ordinary CRUD, but validating a filter's HQL builds the same `ResolutionContext` that `/search/schema` pays for (~8 statements, including a workspace-wide label projection and a full member scan), so creating one with an invalid body was an unthrottled eight-query refusal loop. `ThrottleCoverageTest.theThrottledPathSetIsSealed` **seals the set and prints the propagation checklist when it fails** — that failure message is the authoritative list of what to edit, and it is deliberately not duplicated as a count anywhere, because a number goes stale one entry before the list does. Since HD-182 the coverage half is a **which-controls** question rather than a type question (`everyExpensiveReadHandlerIsAlsoConcurrencyBounded`, plus the async tripwire): the write budget uses the same interceptor type and deliberately carries no occupancy bound, so "there is a `PrincipalThrottleInterceptor` here" stopped implying "this is concurrency-bounded".

Other bounds: `app.reports.max-window-days` (365) refuses an over-long window with a 400 naming the cap — **never a silent clamp** — and separately bounds the burn-up's day series (clipped and disclosed, not refused, because there the caller stated no window: the sprint did). `app.reports.max-rows` (20 000) is the row cap behind `meta.cap`. Velocity's 1–12 sprint sample and Insights' bucket caps are **hard-coded constants on purpose**: twelve sprints is about half a year, and a band computed from further back describes a team that has since changed its members, its process and its definition of a point.

**No status code anywhere depends on a delivery capability** (Rule A). A Kanban project's sprint reports answer exactly as a Scrum project's do, and `measure=POINTS` returns points in a project with `estimation` off. Capabilities narrow what `/search/schema` *offers*; they never narrow what resolves.

## Notifications — a workspace-scoped inbox (HD-135, V20)

**A notification belongs to exactly one workspace, and you see it only while you are a member of that workspace.** Spec: `docs/design/notification-workspace-scoping-proposal.md`; ADR `docs/adr/0009-tenant-scoped-user-inbox.md`. The user-facing contract is `docs/api-cloud.md` / `docs/api-dc.md` → *Notifications* and `openapi.yaml`.

The defect this closes: a notification's `title` and `body` are **denormalised copies of workspace content** — the mentioning user's display name and up to 120 characters of the comment — written into the row at delivery time. `notifications` carried no `workspace_id`, so every finder was keyed on the user alone and there was nothing left to redact at render time. HD-132 is what turned it from latent to live: until member removal existed, the only way to lose access to a workspace was to have the account deleted, which `user_id ON DELETE CASCADE` already handled.

- **`V20__notifications_workspace_scope.sql`** — `workspace_id UUID NOT NULL`, FK to `workspaces(id) ON DELETE CASCADE` (single-column: the parent *is* `workspaces`, so the column already carries tenancy exactly — **do not transplant `V19`'s composite-vs-single argument here**, it was about a parent with no `workspace_id` at all), plus `idx_notifications_workspace`, which exists for the cascade's RI scan and for future per-workspace queries and is **not** the index the read filter uses (that is `idx_notifications_user`). Backfill parses the workspace id out of `link` with a strict-UUID regex and **validates it by joining `workspaces`**, so a row only gets a value that really exists and the FK cannot fail; a row that still resolves to nothing is **copied into `notifications_unresolvable_v20` and then deleted**, as a backstop after a blocking pre-flight, not as a plan. The quarantine table is created **only when the count is non-zero** (a clean install must not inherit an empty artefact of a condition it never met), is mapped and read by nothing, and is the operator's to drop. A non-zero pre-flight count has **two causes with opposite actions** — a link that parsed naming a workspace since removed (deploy; before `V20` there was no FK, so such orphans accumulated silently) versus a link that did not parse at all (deploy too, but report it: that one means an unknown producer). The earlier "non-zero ⇒ do not deploy, reopen the design question" instruction was wrong on both counts and prescribed something no DC operator can do.
- **The predicate lives in the query, in all four finders** — `AND n.workspace.id IN (SELECT m.workspace.id FROM WorkspaceMember m WHERE m.user = :user)`, quoted once as `NotificationRepository.VISIBLE`. Uncorrelated `IN` rather than correlated `EXISTS`, because `markAllReadForUser` is a bulk `@Modifying` UPDATE where the uncorrelated form is unambiguously supported; PostgreSQL plans both as the same semi-join. The filter must be **inside the statement that applies the limit** — `list` caps at 30, so a Java-side filter over the fetched page would show a reader with 30 hidden rows an empty bell.
- **`NotificationRepository`'s only supertype is Spring Data's marker `Repository`**, for the reason `RoleRepository` refuses the same: any richer one contributes an unfiltered `findById`/`findAll` to the single interface whose whole contract is that none exists. Phrase it about the *category*, not about `JpaRepository` — `CrudRepository`, `ListCrudRepository`, `PagingAndSortingRepository`, a custom fragment, and above all `JpaSpecificationExecutor` (not a `JpaRepository` subtype at all, and `findAll(spec)` reads whatever the caller passes) reopen the hole identically. `NotificationFinderSealTest` is three layers: the supertype list is exactly `[Repository]`; every method reachable via `getMethods()` (**not** `getDeclaredMethods()`, which is blind to inheritance) carries **both** halves of the predicate — `n.user = :user` *and* the membership subquery, because a finder with only the tenancy half hands back other members' inboxes; and no file outside the repository names `Notification`/`notifications` as a query target at all. All three were probed by writing the offence and watching the test refuse.
- **`NotificationService.create` takes a `Workspace`, not a `UUID`** — a deliberate deviation from the ticket. It used to take an id lifted from the request path while the row it wrote described an issue resolved two classes away, so "the two agree" was a property of that resolution; passing the entity the domain object already carries makes it a property of the type. `workspace.getId()` still feeds `NotificationRaised`, so the SSE wire contract is byte-for-byte unchanged.
- **API:** `NotificationResponse` gains `workspaceId` (additive), so a client never recovers the tenant by regexing `link` — a rendering detail of whichever producer raised the row. `POST /api/notifications/{id}/read` answers **404** for any notification the caller cannot see; an unknown id, another user's id and a left workspace are one indistinguishable answer, and **no 403 was introduced anywhere**. `read-all` leaves hidden rows **unread** — hiding a row must not mutate it — so rejoining restores the inbox with its read state intact. The promise is *hidden*, not *deleted*; say it that way, because a user re-added the next day will notice which one was true.
- **The feed costs one statement, and that is measured** (`NotificationProxyQueryCountTest`). `Notification.workspace` is `LAZY` with no `JOIN FETCH` and `NotificationResponse.of` reads `getWorkspace().getId()` per row: Hibernate resolves an identifier *getter method* even for a field-access entity and short-circuits it inside the proxy, so the id is served without initialising. Reviewers split on this, so the test creates its rows in a **prior transaction** — a fixture that still holds the `Workspace` answers the question itself and proves nothing. Read any *other* field of that association on this path and the cost stops being fixed. The same mechanism corrects `SprintScopeLedger`'s javadoc, which claimed Hibernate "cannot promise to serve even an identifier without initialising" under field access: it can, verified after `em.clear()` too — the real argument for passing `workspaceId` in is that `getId()` is then the *only* accessor that still works, and nothing marks that boundary for the next reader.
- **`/api/notifications*` has no rate-limit budget, deliberately** — proposal §9.3 records why (work per request below the cheapest throttled surface; the bell fetches once on mount and is then fed by SSE) and names what would flip it. The throttled set is sealed by `ThrottleCoverageTest`, so this is an absence a test knows about rather than one it missed.
- **The SSE half was already closed** and no fix shipped for it: the event is workspace-keyed, `SseRegistry.sendToUser` reads `connections.get(workspaceId)`, subscribing goes through `workspaceAccess.requireMember`, and removal disconnects open streams after commit. What was open is everything **durable** — the row, the list, the count, and `markRead`'s response body.
- **No DC/Cloud difference, and that is the decision, not an omission** — no property, no env var, no profile gate; a toggle here would mean "leak in one mode". The blast radius differs though: in Cloud the workspaces on one instance belong to different paying customers, so the same surviving row is one customer's comment text readable by an account they revoked.

**Known residual, recorded rather than hidden — and it is a coupling, not a leak.** `CommentService.applyMentions` builds its candidate set from *every* workspace member, with no project-access check. That was first written up as "a member can be mentioned into a project they cannot open", and **the premise is false**: the `Permission` catalog has no read/view constant, `WorkspaceAccessService.resolveProject` resolves a non-OPEN project rather than refusing it, and `fallback` returns an empty `PermissionSet` — so a workspace member can already read every project's issues and comments and only lacks the ability to *act*. A mention discloses nothing they could not fetch directly. What is real is the latent coupling: **the day a read permission enters the catalog, the workspace-wide candidate set becomes a disclosure**, and whoever adds it owns narrowing this picker in the same change. This ticket's claim is exactly "workspace membership", and the column added is deliberately `workspace_id`, not `project_id`.

## Write budget & workspace storage quota (HD-191, V26)

Two controls that look alike and defend different things. **The write budget is keyed on the actor** and defends the instance — connection pool, CPU, store requests, SSE and notification fan-out. **The storage quota is keyed on the tenant** and defends the bill and the disk. Neither substitutes for the other: one member can exhaust a shared workspace quota entirely within their own budget, and the quota never sees churn (upload → delete → upload leaves the total where it started while every stored byte in between has been paid for). Removing either leaves a real hole.

**API (new).** `GET /api/workspaces/{wsId}/storage` — any member, unbudgeted, one primary-key read plus two properties; returns `quotaEnabled` and usage, with `quotaBytes`/`availableBytes`/`percentUsed` **`null` rather than a sentinel** when the quota is off, so clients narrow on the flag. `GET /api/workspaces/{wsId}/storage/projects` — `workspace.edit` (real disclosure: project names and volumes), on the **reports** budget, publishing `unattributedBytes` rather than normalising it away. Both resolve through `WorkspaceAccessService.requireMember`, so non-existence and non-membership are both 404; the single 403 in the feature is a proven member without `workspace.edit` on the breakdown.

**API (changed).** `POST …/issues/{n}/attachments` gains **409 `STORAGE_QUOTA_EXCEEDED`** carrying `quotaBytes`, `usedBytes`, `availableBytes` and `fileBytes` — **not** a 429 and with **no `Retry-After`**, because waiting frees no bytes and 413 is already taken twice by the per-file ceilings. Every mutating handler under `…/projects/*/issues/**` gains **429 + `Retry-After`** from the write budget; the upload additionally spends the byte budget, whose 429 is told from the request one by `detail`.

**Config** (`docs/api-dc.md` operator table, `.env.prod.example`, `docs/self-hosting.md`): `WRITE_REQUESTS_PER_MINUTE` (180), `WRITE_UPLOAD_BYTES_PER_MINUTE` (250MB), `STORAGE_QUOTA_ENABLED` (true), `STORAGE_QUOTA_WORKSPACE_BYTES` (100GB base, **10GB in the `cloud` profile** — differs by property, never by code path), `STORAGE_QUOTA_WARN_PERCENT` (80), `STORAGE_QUOTA_RECONCILE_CRON`. The two budgets are under `app.rate-limit.enabled`; **the quota deliberately is not** — folding it in would mean removing a disk bound requires disabling brute-force protection on the login page. That is the second control outside the master switch, and the claim to carry forward is the *property* (a bound with its own switch, or with none, is outside it), not the number.

**Accounting.** Usage is a counter row in `workspace_storage_usage` maintained by a database trigger — so it follows FK cascades the application does not participate in, it is **cluster-wide and exact** rather than node-local like the budgets, and there is no managed entity that can clobber it (the `issue_seq` scar, answered structurally). It counts **rows**, not objects in the store, so a blob orphaned by a failed delete is invisible to it; a reconciler re-derives the total on a schedule and publishes the drift.

**Where this is documented:** `openapi.yaml` (the two operations, `WorkspaceStorageResponse` / `ProjectStorageEntry` / `WorkspaceStorageByProjectResponse` / `StorageQuotaProblemDetail`, the `WriteThrottled` response, the upload's 409/429 and the info-description rate-limit prose) · `docs/api-cloud.md` + `docs/api-dc.md` (*Workspace storage* under Workspaces, the *Attachments* refusal ordering, *Rate limits*, and on DC the six operator rows) · `docs/self-hosting.md` · `.env.prod.example` · `application.properties` + `application-cloud.properties` · `docs/design/write-budget-and-storage-quota-proposal.md`. The authoritative propagation list for a change to a *throttled path* remains `ThrottleCoverageTest.PROPAGATION_CHECKLIST`.

## CI/CD

- CI/CD is two workflows (multi-stage `Dockerfile`). **`build.yml`** — `build-and-test` runs on pushes to `main`/tags and on every PR (builds + tests against a Postgres service container) in **one** `./mvnw -B verify` invocation (which since **HD-242** also runs the frontend `vitest` suite: a fourth `frontend-maven-plugin` execution, `npm-test`, bound to the `test` phase. Before it, every `*.test.ts`/`*.test.tsx` under `src/main/frontend` ran on no automated path at all — `tsc -b` failed the build on a type error and nothing executed a single assertion, so the HD-194 licensing seal could only ever fail a person who remembered to type `npm test`. Bound to `test` rather than an earlier phase so `-DskipTests` switches it off exactly like surefire — which is why the image build in `build-and-push` does not pay it a third time per pipeline — while `-Dfrontend.skip=true` drops all four executions together. Cost ~45-70s per non-skipped build, measured three ways in the pom comment. It is also where HD-240's margin survey reaches the CI log: read the slowest-test percentage printed after the run — above ~50% means the `testTimeout` bound is sized for the wrong machine and wants revisiting, and muting the suite is the one response that just returns us to a guard nobody runs); `build-and-push` then publishes to `ghcr.io/zherikhov/hamstrack` on pushes to `main` and on release tags only, after tests pass; `github-release` finally cuts the GitHub Release for a tag (`softprops/action-gh-release@v2`, `generate_release_notes`, `permissions: contents: write`, `prerelease` auto-set when the tag contains a `-`). It `needs: build-and-push`, so a Release never advertises a version whose image failed to publish; re-running the same tag updates the Release instead of failing, and does not overwrite a hand-edited body. **Release-tag forms:** both `vX.Y.Z` and bare `X.Y.Z` are accepted (`tags: ["v[0-9]*", "[0-9]*"]`) — the repo carries both conventions and every downstream step strips the leading `v`, so the two are interchangeable. *This was a live bug until 2026-08-16: the filter was `v*` only, so tags `0.5.0 … 0.12.5` matched nothing and silently published no image and no Release.* The `[0-9]` anchor is deliberate — a bare `*` would fire the whole pipeline on non-release tags. **Image tags:** `main` → `latest` + `sha-<commit>` (rolling — the owner's prod tracks `latest`); a release tag → `X.Y.Z` + `X.Y` (semver, for self-hosters to pin) **plus `latest`, when the tag is stable and sits on `main`'s tip**. *`latest` used to be main-only (`enable={{is_default_branch}}`) — deliberately, so releases wouldn't move it; HD-115 reversed that because it was the reason a release never reached prod (below).* `latest` now means "the newest build production should run", and is published by exactly two things: a `main` build, and a stable release-tag build. The `enable=` condition is no longer the metadata action's `{{is_default_branch}}` template but a `publish_latest` output computed in the `Determine app version` step, which is `false` for a tag containing `-` (pre-releases publish semver images + a pre-release GitHub Release, never `latest`, never a deploy) and `false` when the tagged commit is not `origin/main`'s tip at build time (**`latest` must only move forward** — tagging an older commit, or `main` moving on mid-build, would otherwise publish an older tree as `latest` and silently roll prod back; the semver images and Release are still published, so a back-tag is pinnable, it just doesn't touch prod). The chosen value + the reason are printed to the job summary. Self-hosting guidance is unchanged: pin `X.Y`, never `latest`. **Versioning is git-tag-driven:** `pom.xml` carries a `0.0.0-DEV` placeholder and is never hand-bumped — `build.yml` computes the version (the tag minus any `v`, else `git describe`) and passes it as the `APP_VERSION` Docker build-arg, which the build stage applies via `versions:set` so `build-info` (About dialog / `/api/meta`) reports it. **To cut a release:** see **`docs/release-checklist.md`**. **Push order is irrelevant since HD-115** — `git push origin main --follow-tags`, or the tag first, or the tag after a PR-button merge, all land the same version on prod, because both the `main` build and the tag build publish `latest` and both deploy. Push `main` first → its build publishes `latest` = `X.Y.Z-N-gsha`, then the tag build overwrites `latest` = `X.Y.Z`; push the tag first → the tag build publishes `X.Y.Z`, and the `main` build on that same commit resolves `git describe` to the exact tag and publishes `X.Y.Z` too. The two Builds sit in different `concurrency` groups and run in parallel, so they may finish in either order — harmless precisely because a tag on main's tip makes both compute the same string, and the forward-only guard covers the case where it doesn't. *The bug this replaced:* the version is baked into the image at build time and prod tracks `latest`, which only `main` builds published, so tagging after pushing `main` produced a correctly-labelled `X.Y.Z` image that prod never pulled while prod kept serving `&lt;previous-tag&gt;-&lt;N&gt;-g&lt;sha&gt;` — on 2026-08-16 prod served `0.12.5-16-g94fac7d` for a 0.13.0 tree. The written procedure ("always push the tag first") was a discipline workaround; one PR-button merge broke it. **Why not "stop baking the version in, inject `APP_VERSION` at deploy time"** (the other candidate fix): it costs a `MetaController`/`BuildProperties` change, a `Dockerfile` change, an `APP_VERSION` var through `docker-compose.prod.yml` + `.env` + `.env.prod.example`, and a checkout + `git describe` inside `deploy.yml` (which does no checkout today) — and it *still* needs the tag build to trigger a deploy, so it does not remove a single edit made here, it adds to them. Worse, it decouples the label from the artifact: the same image could report any version an env var claims (a tag on an older commit would stamp prod's newer code with an older number), and a self-hoster's pinned `X.Y.Z` image would no longer know its own version without being told. Baked-in stays: the image is self-describing, and `latest` moving forward is what keeps label and code honest. **`workflow_dispatch`** was added at the same time: Actions → Build → *Run workflow* rebuilds and re-publishes any ref (main or a tag) without inventing a commit — previously the only rebuild was *Re-run all jobs* on an existing run, which needs that run to exist. A dispatched run publishes and deploys exactly like a push (`build-and-push`'s `if:` and `deploy.yml`'s event check both accept `workflow_dispatch`). In-app, the matching project **version** (HD-32 Releases page, named `X.Y.Z` without the `v`) is marked released around the same time; the tracker version is the plan/scope record, the git tag is the code record. **CI timing & the three things that were slow** (measured 2026-08-16 over 45 runs): a branch/PR run is `build-and-test` only, a `main` run adds `build-and-push` sequentially. `Run tests` dominates — it went **129s (Aug 13) → 306s (Aug 16)**, a 2.4× jump caused by HD-6 landing 21 test classes / +5058 lines in one commit; ~8 min end-to-end is normal for 49 `@SpringBootTest` classes against a live Postgres plus a frontend build plus an image push, so watch the *slope*, not the number. Three fixes applied: (1) **`push` no longer runs on `["**"]`** — every commit on a PR'd branch was validated twice (runs 40/41 on `9ae88a5`, 43/44 on `6cab999`, ~6 min each, identical work); feature branches now go through the `pull_request` trigger, and a branch with no open PR gets no CI. Plus a `concurrency` group that supersedes in-flight runs, explicitly **not** on `main`/tags (cancelling there would cut the `workflow_run` deploy chain). (2) **One `./mvnw -B verify`** instead of `package -DskipTests` + `test`: `frontend-maven-plugin` sits on `generate-resources`, so two invocations built the whole SPA twice (~40-60s; `-Dfrontend.skip=true` could not stop it then — the plugin had no `<skip>` binding, fixed for local loops by HD-94, but it must stay OFF in CI, whose `verify` has to produce the SPA once). (3) **`cache-from/to: type=gha`** on the image build (+`docker/setup-buildx-action`, without which the gha exporter silently no-ops) — the Dockerfile rebuilt deps + npm + vite a third time per pipeline from an empty layer store (~105s). **Not done, deliberately:** parallel surefire forks — the pool-cap comment in `pom.xml` documents the suite already hitting PG `max_connections`, so forks need per-fork schema isolation first. **The one lever that scales:** 19 distinct `@SpringBootTest(properties = …)` signatures exist; 28 classes share one cached context but ~16 signatures are one-offs, each paying a full context boot (~5-8s). **`deploy.yml`** — owner-only; triggered by `workflow_run` after a successful `Build` on `main` **or on a stable release tag** (HD-115 — the old comment "release-tag builds don't deploy, prod already has that code from `main`" was right about the *code* and blind to the *label*: the version is baked in, so the tag build's image is the only one carrying `X.Y.Z`). `workflow_run` has **no `tags:` filter** — a tag-triggered run reports the tag name in `head_branch`, so the trigger filter is `branches: [main, "v[0-9]*", "[0-9]*"]`, and the job `if:` re-checks what a glob can't express: `conclusion == 'success'`, the upstream event is `push`/`workflow_dispatch` (a `pull_request` Build can also carry `head_branch: main` — a fork PR from its own `main` — and must never deploy), and the ref name contains no `-` (keeps pre-release tags off prod). A `concurrency: deploy-production` group with `cancel-in-progress: false` **serialises** deploys, because the `main` and tag Builds can now finish seconds apart and two simultaneous `docker compose up -d` in `/opt/hamstrack` would fight; the second run re-pulls and is a no-op if `latest` didn't move. A final `Report deployed version` step (`continue-on-error`, polls `https://hamstrack.com/api/meta` for up to 2 min) prints what prod actually reports — the HD-115 bug was a wrong *label* on a correct deployment, which nothing in the pipeline ever looked at. It is polled from the runner rather than inside the SSM command because `aws ssm wait command-executed` gives up after ~100s and a slow restart would then fail a good deploy. It reaches the server via **AWS SSM** (SSH port 22 is closed — see `docs/ops-prod-hardening.md` §3), running an `AWS-RunShellScript` command that fetches the repo tree for the built commit from codeload BY SHA and hands it to `ops/deploy/apply-config.sh` (HD-199): **configuration is delivered from the repository at the built commit**, so a merged compose/observability change reaches the box with the image it was built beside — an agreement enforced by the pin check below, not by the sha in the fetch URL, because `APP_IMAGE_TAG` can point the box at an older image and a tag is mutable. What is synced is `ops/deploy/synced-paths.txt`; `.env` and `Caddyfile` are never synced and the script refuses them regardless of the manifest — and refuses them the same way when they appear *inside* a synced directory — which is what lets the image pin live in `APP_IMAGE_TAG` in `.env` and survive every deploy. Four properties of the applier are worth knowing without opening it: **the deploy is all-or-nothing** (the image is pulled only after every file is placed, so a red deploy leaves prod entirely on the image *and* config it had — before this, a half-broken deploy still pulled); **a MOVED `APP_IMAGE_TAG` refuses the deploy** (nothing pins the *config* beside the image, so syncing a newer tree onto a held-back image can re-apply the change an incident was rolling back; the applier compares the `.env` pin with `.deployed-image-tag`, the tag the last apply placed config beside — unchanged proceeds, so a box that pins by policy is not blocked for ever, while a tag that just moved stops every deploy until it is moved back or a hand run adopts it with `--adopt-pin`, which re-stamps — its sibling `--allow-pinned` applies THIS RUN ONLY and deliberately leaves the stamp alone, so an urgent config fix during a rollback cannot quietly tell the next unattended deploy that the held-back image is the intended one; the pin is also read the way Compose reads it, including a pin exported into the environment, which is refused because only `.env` survives a deploy. The three-way decision, both flags and the parser are sealed by `ApplyConfigPinGuardTest`, which drives the real script); **the services whose config is bind-mounted are restarted** when `observability/` changed, because `up -d` compares the service *definition* and a container otherwise keeps the deleted inode of the file just replaced while both drift scopes correctly read 0; and **`COMPOSE_FILES`** narrows the two-file default for a deployment that runs no observability stack (the same variable narrows the drift check). Drift between box and release is a metric (`hamstrack_config_drift`, three scopes) with two warning rules. A red `Build` blocks the deploy. Required repo secrets: `AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID`, `AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY` (region `eu-north-1`; the deploy IAM user may only run `AWS-RunShellScript` on the one instance, whose id is **inlined** in `deploy.yml` — not secret, and a mistyped secret would be masked as `***` and 403). The old SSH-based `SERVER_HOST`/`SERVER_USER`/`SERVER_SSH_PRIVATE_KEY` secrets are obsolete. The server keeps `docker-compose.prod.yml`, `docker-compose.observability.yml`, `observability/`, `ops/`, `Caddyfile` and `.env` in `/opt/hamstrack` — the first four placed by the deploy, the last two owned by the operator. The `app` service is config-lean: it pulls the operator's whole `.env` via `env_file: .env` (so every app var flows through without being re-declared in compose) and sets only two things explicitly under `environment:` — `SPRING_PROFILES_ACTIVE` (the `cloud`/`dc` switch, `${SPRING_PROFILES_ACTIVE:-cloud}`) and the internal `DB_URL` (points at the `postgres` compose service, never overridable). The `postgres` service uses `${DB_USERNAME:?…}`/`${DB_PASSWORD:?…}` fail-fast so a missing credential aborts `up` with a clear message instead of a cryptic later failure. `.env.prod.example` is the full template — it covers every variable the app reads: `GITHUB_OWNER` (lowercase!), `SPRING_PROFILES_ACTIVE` (`cloud`|`dc`), `DB_USERNAME`/`DB_PASSWORD`, `JWT_SECRET`, `SEED_ADMIN_EMAIL` **and** `SEED_ADMIN_PASSWORD` (the admin is seeded only when *both* are set — a blank email skips seeding, see `DataSeeder`; `SEED_ADMIN_DISPLAY_NAME` optional), `APP_BASE_URL` (must be the real public URL — email links, robots/sitemap, OG tags default to `http://localhost:8080` otherwise), `SITE_ADDRESS` (the domain(s) Caddy serves + gets certs for — the `Caddyfile` is templated as `{$SITE_ADDRESS}`, no domain is hardcoded; its host must match `APP_BASE_URL`'s host; fail-fast `${SITE_ADDRESS:?…}` on the caddy service), `MAIL_*` (real SMTP — defaults point at the dev MailHog, so email verification/login fails in prod without it), and storage. **Storage default is profile-driven, not set in compose**: `application-{cloud,dc}.properties` resolve `app.storage.type=${STORAGE_TYPE:s3}` (cloud) / `${STORAGE_TYPE:local}` (dc), so `STORAGE_TYPE` is only needed to override that per-model default; `STORAGE_S3_BUCKET`/`STORAGE_S3_REGION` are required when the effective type is s3. (Do NOT hardcode `STORAGE_TYPE` in compose — a forced value defeats the dc profile's local default.) The `app` service has a healthcheck (`wget` the public `/api/meta`, `start_period: 40s` for Spring Boot startup) and `caddy` waits on `condition: service_healthy`, so the proxy comes up only once the app is actually serving (no 502 window on `up`). Local-storage attachments persist in the `attachments_data` volume. Prod is served at https://hamstrack.com — DNS on Cloudflare (A records for `@`/`www`, **DNS only** / grey cloud; switching to the orange-cloud proxy requires SSL mode Full (strict)); Caddy auto-manages Let's Encrypt certs (persisted in the `caddy_data` volume). If the ghcr package is private, the server needs a one-time `docker login ghcr.io` with a PAT (`read:packages`).
