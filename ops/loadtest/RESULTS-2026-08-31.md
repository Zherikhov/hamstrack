# Load capacity measurement — RESULTS, 2026-08-31

**One sentence:** on the production `t3.small` this deployment serves **45 concurrent
browsing users at p95 364 ms**, saturates at **~43 requests/second**, and the resource that
runs out is **memory** — never CPU, which never came close.

> **CORRECTION, 2026-09-01 (HD-179/HD-180/HD-225).** The **JVM max heap** row above is a
> twelfth harness defect of §9's family, and the row is wrong. `fingerprint.sh` read it with
> `docker exec … java -XX:+PrintFlagsFinal -version`, which starts a **second JVM** that does
> not inherit the entrypoint's arguments: what it printed is that fresh process's own default
> ergonomics — `MaxRAMPercentage=25` of the 1 GiB cgroup limit, i.e. `268435456` — and not
> the application's. The application runs the image's
> `-XX:MaxRAMPercentage=50.0` (`Dockerfile` ENTRYPOINT since 0.17.0/HD-152, confirmed in
> `/proc/1/cmdline` on this box on 2026-08-28), which against the same 1 GiB limit resolves
> to **536870912 — 512 MB**. `docs/self-hosting.md` had already written down why the flag
> must be repeated on an `exec`; the fingerprint did not repeat it. The corroborating probe
> that would have caught it — `jvm_memory_max_bytes` from the actuator — was scraped on port
> **8080**, so it returned nothing (defect 9's port, in a second script). Both sources of
> truth failed the same way, and the wrong one was the one that answered.
>
> **What changes:** nothing in §3, §4 or §5. The attribution is *more* consistent, not less —
> a 512 MB heap explains the app's measured ~678 MB peak RSS far better than a 256 MB one
> would, and a heap twice this box's spare memory is a better account of 4.99 s GC pauses at
> 147 MB available. Read every "256 MB heap" in this document as **512 MB, and as a
> declaration rather than a measurement**: this window did not measure it. From 0.18.0 the
> application logs its own resolved ceiling once at startup (`Memory: max heap … bytes …`),
> which is now the first thing `fingerprint.sh` records and the only artefact here that
> cannot be reading a different process.
>
> **And the collector was never recorded at all, which is the same defect one level down.**
> `MaxRAMPercentage` was measured on the wrong process; the garbage collector was not
> measured on any. It matters here more than the heap figure does: at a **1 GiB** container
> limit the JVM is below its "server-class machine" threshold and ergonomically selects
> **SerialGC** — single-threaded, stop-the-world — while at 2 GiB, same image and same
> flags, it selects **G1** (measured 2026-09-01 in containers on `eclipse-temurin:21-jre-alpine`
> — the tag `Dockerfile`'s runtime stage uses — with 2 CPUs and the image's own
> `-XX:MaxRAMPercentage=50.0`; at 512m it is SerialGC again).
> This box ran `mem_limit: 1g`, so §3.7's **4.99 s pause** was almost certainly a serial
> full GC of a 512 MB heap on a swapping host, and not a G1 pause of any kind. That is a
> different remedy from "the heap is too small": a bigger `APP_MEMORY_LIMIT` fixes it partly
> by giving the heap room and partly by crossing the threshold that changes the collector.
> **Not re-attributed retroactively** — nothing in this window observed the collector, so
> this is a property of the configuration, recorded so the next window can measure it. From
> 0.18.0 the startup line names the collector, so it will be in the fingerprint.
>
> **The startup line's byte figure is `MaxHeapSize` itself, not `Runtime.maxMemory()`**,
> which is the same trap a third time: under SerialGC the JVM's *usable* heap at a 1 GiB
> limit is `518979584`, 3.4% under the `536870912` this document, `.env.prod.example`,
> `docs/self-hosting.md` and `docs/observability.md` all state. A 3.4% gap reads as rounding
> rather than as a different quantity, in the one artefact the harness now treats as
> authoritative.
>
> The PostgreSQL settings in this section are **not** affected — they were read from
> `pg_settings` on the running server, not from a second process.

Every stage below: **zero 5xx, zero rate-limit refusals, zero unexpected 404s, zero canary

**Throughput plateaus at ~43 req/s**: 43.4 at 60 VU, 42.0 at 80 VU — more concurrency buys

| VU | requests | req/s | median | p95 |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| **45** | **857** | **2.6** | **20 ms** | **1009 ms** |

The ladder was capped at 45 for this mix and the next, because 60 had already been shown to
breach on memory and the ceiling is shared. Request rates are low by construction: the
per-principal budgets (120 search/min, 60 reports/min) and the mix's think time bound it.
p95 grows 4.4× from 1 → 45 VU while the median barely moves — the cost is in the tail, and
the tail is GC (§3.7).

### 3.3 Writing — **no throughput ceiling to 45 VU; a tail artefact at 30**

| VU | requests | req/s | median | p95 | max | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 114 | 0.4 | 6 ms | 71 ms | — | passed |
| 5 | 285 | 0.9 | 9 ms | 29 ms | — | passed |
| 15 | 715 | 2.3 | 8 ms | 20 ms | 144 ms | passed |
| 30 | 1 363 | 4.3 | 9 ms | 27 ms | **5 s** | **breached** `p99 < 1500` |
| 45 | 2 006 | 6.3 | 8 ms | 17 ms | 146 ms | passed |

**Non-monotonic, therefore not a ceiling.** At 30 VU the median (9 ms) and p95 (27 ms) are
indistinguishable from 15 and 45; only the tail moved, and it moved to five seconds. A
capacity limit does not behave this way — 45 would have been worse, and it was better. The
cause is measured in §3.7. **Writes are cheap on this deployment**; what hurts them is an
occasional multi-second stall that has nothing to do with writing.

### 3.4 Soak — **NOT RUN**

The token chain was spent (§9), and repairing it is a 25-minute re-mint. Judged not worth
the window time against eight stages of five-minute sustained holds already recorded. **This
is a gap in the evidence, not a passed check**: nothing here establishes fifteen minutes of
steady-state stability, and criterion 14's soak clause is unmet.

### 3.5 Tenancy canary — **clean**

`hs_canary_leak = 0` on **every stage of every mix**, and `hs_unexpected_404 = 0`. The
premise was verified independently before the window: a workspace that exists but is not
ours and a workspace that does not exist both answer **404 with the same body** (`"Workspace
not found"`), differing only in `instance`, which echoes the caller's own request URI and
therefore discloses nothing.

### 3.6 Did the harness saturate itself?

Not in the mixes. The generator is a c7i.large carrying at most 100 VUs and 620 kB/s; its
`dropped_iterations` was **0** in every ladder stage. It did appear in **P1** — 28 dropped
iterations — but there the cause is the system under test returning in 16 s, not the
generator failing to send.

### 3.7 The resource that ran out, measured

| signal | value |
|---|---|
| Max JVM GC pause during the write mix | **4.986 s** |
| Max slowest request, same window | **5.0 s** |
| Peak major page faults | **~300/s** |
| Swap in use | 51 MB at the start → **275 MB** at the end |
| Host available memory at the breach | **147 MB** (floor 150, alert threshold 200) |
| OOM kills | **0** |

The 4.99 s GC pause and the 5.0 s slowest request are the same event. A 256 MB heap on a box
whose free memory is measured in tens of megabytes means the collector's own working set is
paged out; a collection that normally costs milliseconds costs five seconds. **This single
mechanism explains both the browse breach at 60 VU and the write tail at 30 VU.** CPU was
never the constraint: credits were `unlimited` and unspent, and load average peaked briefly
at 7.3 only while memory was already exhausted.

## 4. The standing predictions (criteria 17, 18)

### P1 — the entitlement probe (HD-182's premise): **confirmed, and worse than predicted**

P1 has one principal work at exactly the rate the product grants it — 120 searches and 60
reports per minute — while a second principal browses normally, and asks whether the second
one suffers.

It never reached the intended arrival rate. k6 aborted after 32 s on `dropped_iterations`
(28), because responses were taking 16 s: median **3.92 s**, p95 **16.26 s**, max **21.95 s**.
The victim's browse numbers are consequently empty — the probe could not run long enough to
produce them.

**The abort is the result.** A single principal, violating nothing, saturates the instance.
The number that explains it: `hs_response_bytes` p90 **and** max are both **1 277 880 bytes**
— the entitled endpoints return ~1.3 MB per response, so 120/min is **~2.6 MB/s of JSON
serialisation against a 256 MB heap**. The per-principal rate limits are set far above what
this deployment can serve; they bound *requests*, not *work*, and here they do not protect
the instance at all.

### P2 — the report heap costing: **did not measure what it exists to measure**

Two reasons, both recorded rather than worked around. First, no row-level report can reach
the cap at this fixture shape (§2), so the capped case — the expensive case P2 is about —
does not occur. Second, P2 drove ~544 iterations/s against a 2/s per-principal budget, so
**91.55 % of its requests (463 421 of 506 187) were refused 429**. It exited 0. That pass
means "the instance refuses quickly" (median 7.18 ms, p95 25.38 ms, zero 5xx) — a real and
mildly reassuring fact, and not the one asked for.

### L — the limiter probe: **NOT RUN**

Its 429 shape is unverified by this probe. Partially covered incidentally: P2 produced
463 421 refusals with no 5xx and no latency cost, so the refusal path is at least cheap and
stable under volume.

### The finding that does not depend on any of the above

**This deployment is memory-bound and nothing else.** Every number in §3.7 says so, and the
remedy is a resource change, not a code change. That is HD-189's subject, and this run is
the input it was waiting for. Concretely: the app has a 256 MB heap because
`MaxRAMPercentage=25` of a 1 GiB container limit, on a 1909 MB host that also runs PostgreSQL
**with no limit at all** plus eight observability containers.

## 9. What the harness got wrong

Every defect below is in the harness or the runbook and **none is in the product**. Each time
a check failed, the application turned out to be right. The numbered list is the set found
before and during the window; one more was found afterwards — the JVM heap figure read off a
second process — and it is recorded where it does its damage, in §1's correction box, rather
than renumbered in here. Do not read the length of this list as the size of the family.

Fixed before the window (commit `cc93288`):

1. **All 14 scripts were committed `100644`.** The runbook has the operator type
   `bash fixture/…`, which masked it — but `teardown.sh` invokes `completeness.sh` directly
   at two call sites, so on any fresh checkout the teardown could delete the fixture and then
   fail to prove it had.
2. **`config.env.example` did not say to quote the bcrypt hash.** The file is *sourced* under
   `set -u` and a bcrypt hash always contains `$`; the documented sequence dies with
   `line 5: $2: unbound variable`.
3. **`20-resync.sql` counted comments without `deleted_at IS NULL`**, so it could not use the
   partial index and became one sequential scan per issue — cancelled after **25 minutes**;
   **345 ms** with the predicate.
4. **Four of five direct `curl_auth` callers omitted the base URL**, dying with "URL rejected:
   No host part" inside `$(…)` whose empty output was then interpreted — so they reported as
   *fixture* failures ("issue history is EMPTY", "NO report reached REPORTS_MAX_ROWS").
5. **The sprint check iterated a paged envelope with `.[]`**, counting object values.
6. **`search/suggest?field=status`** asserted 200 on one of four fields that answer 422 by
   design.
7. **`cycle-time?from=1970-01-01`** — 20 696 days against a 365-day maximum, so a 400 every
   time.
8. **Attachment upload used `.bin`**, outside the extension whitelist: 415 ×3, with a message
   that asserted three orphaned objects which were never written.

Found during the window (fix uncommitted, in this commit):

9. **`capture.sh` scraped the wrong port.** Its default is `localhost:8080`; Hamstrack serves
   the actuator on its **management port, 9090** (`management.server.port=${MANAGEMENT_PORT:9090}`),
   so it collected **nothing on every install of this product**. Worse, the fail-fast for an
   unreachable actuator existed **only in `LOAD_CAPTURE_MODE=host`** — the mode nothing uses —
   while `docker` mode checked that the container *name* resolved and then trusted the URL.
   Caught in pre-flight; without it the window would have produced zero application metrics.
   Both the default and the missing probe are fixed.
10. **`mint-tokens.js` records `mintedAt` as the moment minting *finished*, but issues tokens
    progressively over ~24 minutes** (it self-throttles to stay under the 15/min per-IP auth
    budget). So the first account's 30-minute token expires ~6 minutes after the file is
    written, while every expiry guard computes from `mintedAt`. The first ladder attempt died
    at stage 2 with `setup: GET /api/workspaces returned 401`, 40 seconds after the earliest
    token lapsed. **The workaround is one line and belongs in the runbook: run
    `refresh-tokens.js` immediately after minting** — it spends the whole chain in 47 seconds
    (`/api/auth/refresh` is deliberately outside the auth limiter), after which every token
    shares one clock and the guard is honest. It then worked exactly as designed, refreshing
    mid-ladder at 07:51 without intervention.
11. **The watchdog's stop file cannot reach the ladder.** `run-ladder.sh` honours it only if
    `LOAD_ABORT_FILE` is set, and the file is written **on the box** while the ladder runs
    **on the generator** — different machines, no shared filesystem. The mechanism is
    structurally inoperative in the two-machine topology the runbook prescribes. Observed:
    the watchdog aborted at 08:20:57 and the ladder started an **80 VU** stage at 08:25:13
    regardless. The README already says the operator is the link; the variable exists as
    though it were not. The operator caught it two minutes in, and nothing was harmed.

**The pattern, stated once because it recurs.** Most of these are *checks that pass without
checking*: a completeness assertion that could only fail, a threshold on an endpoint that can
only refuse, a scrape of a port nothing listens on, a fail-fast installed on the path nobody
runs, an assertion against the wrong JSON shape, a probe measuring a limiter instead of the
thing behind it — and the twelfth, a heap reading taken from a JVM that was not the
application's. They are invisible in a green run. Every one was found by
executing the procedure against production rather than reading it.
